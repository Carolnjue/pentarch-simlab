    Decibels: the language of the desk

    Audio levels span an enormous range, so engineers use the logarithmic decibel. For amplitude-like quantities (voltage, sample value) the level relative to a reference is

    $$L = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{A}{A_\text{ref}}\right)\ \text{dB},\qquad L_P = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P}{P_\text{ref}}\right)\ \text{dB}$$

    so doubling the amplitude adds about $6.02$ dB and doubling the power adds about $3.01$ dB. A fader at $-6$ dB halves the signal amplitude. Every digital meter on this desk reads in dBFS (decibels relative to full scale): $0$ dBFS is the largest sample value a fixed-point converter can represent, so all real levels are negative. Analogue desks use dBu ($0$ dBu $= 0.775$ V RMS); the EBU alignment level of $0$ dBu maps to $-18$ dBFS, which is why $-18$ dBFS is the classic gain-staging target.

    Two uncorrelated signals of equal level sum to $+3$ dB (powers add); two identical (correlated) signals sum to $+6$ dB (amplitudes add). That is why ten channels each peaking at $-18$ dBFS can still drive the mix bus towards $-6$ dBFS.

    Signal flow through a channel strip

    Each channel on this console processes the signal in a fixed order, just like an analogue or digital desk: input trim → high-pass filter → EQ → compressor (+ make-up) → mute → fader → pan → mix bus. The monitor send taps the signal before the fader (pre-fader), so moving a fader for the audience does not change what the band hears in their wedges. Reverb and delay sends are post-fader, so the effect level follows the fader. See the Signal flow tab for the full diagram.

    Equalisation

    High-pass filter (HPF): removes energy below the cut-off frequency $f_c$ with a slope of 12 dB/octave here (2nd-order Butterworth, $Q=0.707$). Use it on everything that does not need deep bass: vocals, keys, pads, guitars, hi-hats.

    Shelving EQ (HF, LF): lifts or cuts everything above (HF, 8 kHz) or below (LF, 100 Hz) the corner frequency by a fixed amount.

    Peaking / bell EQ (HM, LM): boosts or cuts a band centred on $f_0$. Its width is set by $Q$; the bandwidth in octaves $N$ relates to $Q$ by

    $$Q = \frac{\sqrt{2^N}}{2^N-1}$$

    so $Q\approx1.41$ is one octave. The mids on this desk are sweepable (you choose $f_0$) with $Q = 1.2$.

    The yellow curve in the analyser is the exact combined magnitude response of the selected channel’s HPF and four bands, computed from the same digital biquad filters that process the audio.

    Compression

    A compressor turns down signals above its threshold $T$. With ratio $R$, the static (steady-state) curve is

    $$L_\text{out} = \begin{cases} L_\text{in}, & L_\text{in} \le T\\[2pt] T + \dfrac{L_\text{in}-T}{R}, & L_\text{in} > T\end{cases}\qquad \text{GR} = (L_\text{in}-T)\left(1-\frac1R\right)$$

    Attack is how quickly gain reduction (GR) is applied after the signal crosses the threshold; release is how quickly it recovers. Fast attack tames transients (and can dull a kick); slow attack lets the transient through. Because compression lowers peaks, you add make-up gain to restore level, which raises the quieter parts: the net effect is a smaller dynamic range. A ratio of $\infty:1$ with a very fast attack is a limiter, used on the master here to stop the output exceeding the ceiling. (The desk uses the browser’s built-in compressor, which has a soft knee and a few ms of look-ahead, so measured GR differs slightly from the hard-knee formula.)

    Panning laws

    A pan pot splits a mono signal between left and right. To keep the perceived loudness constant as a source moves, consoles use a constant-power law. With pan position $p\in[-1,1]$ and $\theta = (p+1)\tfrac{\pi}{4}$:

    $$g_L = \cos\theta,\qquad g_R = \sin\theta,\qquad g_L^2+g_R^2 = 1$$

    At centre each side is $\cos 45^\circ = 0.707$, i.e. $-3$ dB: the “−3 dB pan law” used by this desk. Some consoles use $-4.5$ or $-6$ dB centre laws ($-6$ dB keeps mono sums constant). Keep kick, snare, bass and lead vocal central; spread hats, percussion, guitars and keys to create width.

    Loudness and broadcast standards

    Peak meters show how close you are to clipping, not how loud a mix sounds. Loudness meters (ITU-R BS.1770) first apply K-weighting (a high-frequency shelf of about $+4$ dB above 2 kHz plus a high-pass around 38 Hz, approximating the ear), then compute the mean square over a window:

    $$\text{LUFS} = -0.691 + 10\log_{10}\sum_{i} G_i\,\overline{z_i^2}$$

    where $\overline{z_i^2}$ is the mean square of channel $i$ and $G_i = 1$ for left and right. Momentary loudness uses a 400 ms window, short-term 3 s, and integrated loudness averages the whole programme with gating (blocks quieter than $-70$ LUFS, and then blocks more than 10 LU below the average of the remaining blocks, are ignored). Targets: EBU R128 broadcast $-23$ LUFS (Kenyan and many African broadcasters follow EBU or ATSC practice), ATSC A/85 $-24$ LKFS, most streaming platforms about $-14$ LUFS, podcasts about $-16$ LUFS. The meter on this page is a simplified real-time approximation, accurate to roughly ±1 LU.

    1. Fader moves in decibels

    A vocal peaks at $-12$ dBFS with the fader at $0$ dB. You pull the fader to $-6$ dB. New peak: $-12 + (-6) = -18$ dBFS. Amplitude ratio: $10^{-6/20} = 0.501$, so the waveform is half as tall.

    Try it: solo the Vocal, switch meters to Post-fader, and watch the peak number under the fader drop by exactly the fader setting.

    2. Summing channels

    Two uncorrelated channels each at $-18$ dBFS RMS: total power $2\times10^{-18/10}$, so $L = 10\log_{10}(2\times 10^{-1.8}) = -18 + 3.01 = -14.99$ dBFS.

    Ten such channels: $-18 + 10\log_{10}10 = -8$ dBFS RMS. This is why individual channels need headroom.

    3. Compressor gain reduction

    Threshold $T=-24$ dBFS, ratio $4:1$, input peak $-12$ dBFS.

    1. Overshoot: $-12-(-24) = 12$ dB.
    2. Output: $-24 + 12/4 = -21$ dBFS.
    3. Gain reduction: $12\times(1-\tfrac14) = 9$ dB.
    4. A quiet passage at $-30$ dBFS is below threshold and stays at $-30$. The difference between loud and quiet shrinks from 18 dB to 9 dB.
    5. Add 6 dB of make-up gain to bring the loud parts back up to about $-15$ dBFS.

    The mini graph in the analyser’s top-right corner draws this curve for the selected channel; the dot shows the live input level.

    4. Pan law

    Pan pot at $p = 0.5$ (half right): $\theta = 1.5\times 45^\circ = 67.5^\circ$. $g_L = \cos67.5^\circ = 0.383$ ($-8.3$ dB), $g_R = \sin 67.5^\circ = 0.924$ ($-0.7$ dB). Check: $0.383^2+0.924^2 = 1.00$, so the total power is unchanged.

    5. Hitting a loudness target

    Your mix measures $-19.5$ LUFS integrated and the platform target is $-14$ LUFS. You need $+5.5$ dB. If the true peak is already $-4$ dBFS, adding 5.5 dB would push peaks to $+1.5$ dBFS, so the limiter must reduce peaks by at least 1.5 dB to stay below 0 dBFS, or 2.5 dB for a $-1$ dBTP ceiling. Loudness is gained at the cost of crest factor (peak-to-loudness ratio).

    6. HPF slope

    A 12 dB/octave HPF at $f_c = 160$ Hz: at 80 Hz (one octave down) the response is about $-12$ dB (exactly $-12.3$ dB for a Butterworth), at 40 Hz about $-24$ dB. Set the Keys HPF to 160 Hz and look at the yellow curve to confirm.

    Free experiments

    1. Pre vs post-fader. Turn up the Vocal’s MON send, switch Listen to Stage monitor, then pull the Vocal fader down. The monitor mix does not change: MON is pre-fader.
    2. Post-fader effects. Give the Snare some REV send. Pull its fader down: the reverb disappears with it, because REV is post-fader.
    3. SIP vs PFL. Solo the Bass in SIP mode (solo-in-place mutes everything else in the main mix). Switch to PFL: the main mix keeps playing, and only your listening feed changes. Broadcast engineers use PFL so the audience never hears a solo.
    4. Attack time. Compress the Kick hard (threshold −40, ratio 8). Try ATK 0.5 ms vs 40 ms and listen to the click of the beater appear and disappear.
    5. Mono compatibility. Pan Keys hard left and Guitar hard right and watch the correlation meter; then pan both centre.
    6. Loudness vs peaks. Set the master limiter ceiling to −6 dB and push the master fader to +8: the integrated loudness rises while the peaks stay pinned. Listen for the loss of punch.

    Aux send
    A separate, adjustable feed of a channel to a bus (monitor wedge, reverb, delay).
    Bus
    A mixing point where several signals are summed (mix bus, aux bus, PFL bus).
    Clipping
    Distortion when a signal exceeds the maximum level (0 dBFS in digital).
    Crest factor / PLR
    Difference between peak level and average (RMS) or loudness level, in dB.
    dBFS / dBu / dBSPL
    Decibels relative to digital full scale / 0.775 V / 20 µPa sound pressure.
    Foldback
    Monitor mixes for performers on stage; normally pre-fader.
    Gain staging
    Setting levels at each point of the chain so signals sit well above the noise floor and well below clipping.
    Gain reduction (GR)
    How much a compressor or limiter is currently turning the signal down, in dB.
    Headroom
    The margin between the operating level and the clipping point.
    HPF / LPF
    High-pass / low-pass filter: passes frequencies above / below the cut-off.
    Insert
    A point where a processor is placed in series with the channel.
    K-weighting
    The frequency weighting used for loudness measurement (BS.1770).
    LUFS / LU
    Loudness units relative to full scale; 1 LU = 1 dB difference in loudness.
    Make-up gain
    Gain added after a compressor to restore level.
    Mud
    Excess energy around 200–500 Hz that makes a mix sound thick and unclear.
    PFL / AFL
    Pre-fade listen / after-fade listen: hear a channel in headphones without affecting the main mix.
    Q
    Quality factor: the sharpness of a filter, centre frequency divided by bandwidth.
    SIP
    Solo-in-place: soloing mutes all other channels in the main mix (studio use only).
    Sibilance / harshness
    Excess energy at 2–8 kHz in vocals (“s” sounds, nasal or edgy tone).
    Trim
    The input gain control at the top of the strip.
    VU
    Volume unit meter: averaging meter with 300 ms ballistics; 0 VU ≈ +4 dBu (pro) or −18 dBFS (EBU alignment).
