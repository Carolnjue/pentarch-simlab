Chromosomes and ploidy

A chromosome is one continuous DNA molecule packaged with proteins (chromatin). Human somatic cells are diploid: 46 chromosomes in 23 homologous pairs, one member of each pair from each parent. Gametes are haploid with 23. We write the number of chromosome sets as $n$ and the DNA content as $C$ (one haploid genome = 1C, about $3.1\times10^9$ bp in humans):

$$\text{somatic G1: } 2n,\ 2C \quad\xrightarrow{\text{S phase}}\quad 2n,\ 4C \quad\xrightarrow{\text{mitosis}}\quad 2\times(2n,\ 2C)$$
$$2n,\ 4C \ \xrightarrow{\text{meiosis I}}\ 2\times(n,\ 2C)\ \xrightarrow{\text{meiosis II}}\ 4\times(n,\ 1C)$$

After replication each chromosome consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere, where the kinetochore assembles. Chromosomes are classified by centromere position: metacentric (arms equal, e.g. 1, 3), submetacentric (e.g. 4–12, X) and acrocentric (very short p arm, e.g. 13–15, 21, 22). The short arm is p, the long arm q, and the ends are protected by telomeres (TTAGGG repeats).

The cell cycle and mitosis

Interphase (G1, S, G2) occupies most of the cycle. Checkpoints at G1/S, G2/M and the spindle assembly checkpoint in metaphase ensure DNA is intact and every kinetochore is attached before anaphase. Mitosis separates sister chromatids so that each daughter cell receives an identical copy of the genome. It is used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.

  1. Prophase: chromatin condenses (condensin), centrosomes separate, spindle forms.
  2. Prometaphase: nuclear envelope breaks down; microtubules capture kinetochores.
  3. Metaphase: chromosomes align at the equator; sister kinetochores bi-oriented.
  4. Anaphase: separase cleaves cohesin; sisters move to opposite poles (anaphase A) and poles move apart (anaphase B).
  5. Telophase and cytokinesis: nuclei re-form; a contractile ring of actin and myosin divides the cytoplasm (a cell plate in plants).

Meiosis: making haploid, varied gametes

Meiosis I is the reductional division: homologues pair, recombine and separate. Meiosis II is equational, like mitosis, separating sister chromatids. Genetic variation arises from three sources:

  • Crossing over in prophase I exchanges segments between non-sister chromatids at chiasmata. Genes far apart on a chromosome recombine more often; the recombination frequency between two loci gives the genetic map distance (1 % = 1 centimorgan).
  • Independent assortment: each bivalent orients at random at metaphase I, giving $2^n$ combinations of whole chromosomes, $2^{23} = 8\,388\,608$ for a human gamete.
  • Random fertilisation of any egg by any sperm squares this: $2^{23}\times2^{23} \approx 7\times10^{13}$ chromosome combinations before counting crossovers.

Chiasmata also hold homologues together until anaphase I; a bivalent without a crossover is prone to mis-segregation. Cohesin along the arms is removed in anaphase I, while centromeric cohesin is protected by shugoshin until anaphase II.

Nondisjunction and aneuploidy

Nondisjunction is the failure of chromosomes to separate. In meiosis I, both homologues go to the same pole; all four gametes are abnormal (two $n+1$, two $n-1$). In meiosis II, sisters fail to separate in one cell, giving one $n+1$, one $n-1$ and two normal gametes. Fertilisation of an $n+1$ gamete gives a trisomy (2n + 1); an $n-1$ gamete gives a monosomy (2n − 1).

1. Chromosome number and DNA content

A human cell in G1 has 46 chromosomes and 2C ≈ 6.2 × 10⁹ bp. After S phase: 46 chromosomes, 92 chromatids, 4C.

After meiosis I: each cell has 23 chromosomes (each still two chromatids), 2C. After meiosis II: 23 chromosomes, 23 chromatids, 1C.

In the 2n = 4 model above: interphase after S = 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids; telophase I = 2 chromosomes, 4 chromatids per cell; each gamete = 2 chromatids.

2. Combinations from independent assortment

Each of $n$ bivalents has 2 orientations, so a gamete can receive $2^n$ combinations of parental chromosomes.

$n = 3$: $2^3 = 8$. Probability that a gamete carries only maternal chromosomes: $(1/2)^3 = 0.125$.

Humans, $n = 23$: $2^{23} = 8\,388\,608$; $P(\text{all maternal}) = 2^{-23} \approx 1.2\times10^{-7}$.

A zygote: $2^{23}\times2^{23} = 2^{46} \approx 7.0\times10^{13}$ combinations, not counting crossing over.

3. Recombination and map distance

In the simulator, a single crossover between the centromere and locus A in a bivalent makes 2 of the 4 chromatids recombinant for that interval, so a crossover in every meiosis gives a recombination frequency of 50 %.

If crossovers in that interval occur in only 20 % of meioses, recombinant gametes are $0.20 \times 0.5 = 10\,\%$: a map distance of 10 cM. This is why recombination frequency never exceeds 50 %.

4. How much DNA, and how tightly packed?

A diploid human nucleus holds about $6.2\times10^9$ bp. At 0.34 nm per bp: $6.2\times10^9 \times 0.34\times10^{-9}\ \text{m} \approx 2.1$ m of DNA.

One nucleosome per ≈200 bp (147 bp core + ≈50 bp linker): $6.2\times10^9/200 \approx 3\times10^7$ nucleosomes per cell.

Chromosome 1 (248 Mb) is $248\times10^6\times0.34$ nm ≈ 8.4 cm long as naked DNA, but about 10 µm as a metaphase chromosome: a packing ratio near $8.4\times10^{-2}/10^{-5} \approx 8000$.

5. Products of nondisjunction

Meiosis I error for chromosome 21 in the mother: eggs with 24 (two copies of 21) or 22 (no 21) chromosomes. Fertilised by a normal sperm (23): 47,+21 (Down syndrome) or 45,−21 (not viable).

Meiosis II error: 1 egg 24, 1 egg 22, 2 eggs normal (23). Only half the products are abnormal.

Paternal meiosis I error for XY: sperm XY or O. XY + X egg → 47,XXY (Klinefelter); O + X egg → 45,X (Turner).
