What happens in a live football gallery

A live match is produced from an outside broadcast (OB) truck parked at the stadium. Inside, the production gallery faces a wall of monitors called the multiviewer. Every camera, the replay servers and the graphics machine appear as tiles, with two large monitors: Preview (PVW), what the director is about to take, and Programme (PGM), what viewers see right now. Coloured borders called tally show status: red means on air, green means on preview. The same tally lights up on the camera itself so the operator knows they are live and must not reframe abruptly.

Director / vision mixer. Chooses which camera is on air and when. In football the director usually operates the vision mixer (switcher) personally: select the next source on the preview bus, then CUT, MIX or WIPE it to programme. They call the camera operators and the replay operators over talkback.
Producer. Owns the editorial content and the running order: when to go to the break, which incidents deserve a replay, which graphics run and when the programme ends. In sport the producer and director work side by side, and the producer often calls replays and graphics.
Sound supervisor. Mixes commentary, crowd effects microphones, the referee microphone, stadium PA, replay audio and music into a clean programme mix at the right loudness. Also provides talkback and the commentators’ foldback.
Graphics operator. Fires on-screen graphics from a template system populated with live match data: the scorebug, lower-third captions, goal, booking and substitution straps, and full-frame statistics.

Other people you would hear on talkback

  • EVS / replay operators record every camera continuously, mark in and out points around incidents and play clips back in slow motion when the director calls “roll replay”.
  • Camera operators (main wide on the gantry, tight follow, behind-goal, handheld RF cameras pitch-side), each listening to the director.
  • Vision engineers (racks/shaders) match exposure and colour of all cameras from remote control panels so cuts are seamless.
  • Floor manager and match manager at pitch level: the link to the referee, stadium staff and the fourth official’s substitution board.
  • Transmission (TX) / master control at the broadcaster, which takes the gallery output, inserts commercial breaks and sends the signal to air.

The vision mixer, step by step

  1. Preview. Press a source on the PVW bus (keys 1–8). Its tile turns green and it appears on the Preview monitor.
  2. Take. Space performs a CUT: preview and programme swap instantly (a “flip-flop” mixer). Enter performs an AUTO transition at the chosen rate; the T-bar does the same by hand.
  3. Rate in frames. Kenyan and most African broadcasters use 25 frames per second, so a 25-frame mix lasts 1 s and a 12-frame mix 0.48 s.
  4. Hot-punch. Pressing a key on the PGM bus (or Shift+number) cuts that source straight to air without preview. Fast, but risky: you have not seen the shot.

The 180° rule in football

Viewers build a mental map: in the first half, team A attacks left to right. All the main cameras sit on one side of the pitch, the gantry side, so that direction never flips. The imaginary line along the length of the pitch is the action axis. Cutting live to a camera on the opposite side (a reverse angle) flips the direction of play on screen and instantly disorients viewers: an attack appears to be going backwards.

Cutting grammar

  • Cut on the stoppage, not the action. Go tight when the ball goes dead; be back on the wide shot before it is live again. Restarts on a close-up are a classic error.
  • Motivated cuts. Every cut should answer a question the viewer has: who scored, how did they react, what did the replay show?
  • Shot length. Cutting faster than about 1.5 s feels jumpy; holding a close-up too long makes viewers feel they are missing the game.
  • Replay grammar. Replays go in and out with a branded sting (wipe) so viewers always know “this is not live”. After a goal the usual order is: wide for the goal itself, ISO on the scorer, crowd or bench cutaway, then replays from two angles before the restart.
  • Never miss the goal. If you are on a cutaway when a shot comes in, you have missed the most important moment of the broadcast. Return to live play early.

From camera to viewer: the OB signal chain

Why everything must be in sync

A vision mixer can only cut cleanly between sources whose frames start at exactly the same instant. A sync pulse generator (genlock: black burst or tri-level sync) locks every camera and device to one reference, and timecode stamps each frame so EVS clips and logs line up. Audio has its own delays: video processing adds latency, so the sound desk delays audio to keep lip-sync within a frame or so.

Key and fill

A graphic is sent as a full-colour fill signal plus a greyscale key (alpha). The keyer computes each output pixel as

$$V_{out} = \alpha\,V_{fill} + (1-\alpha)\,V_{background},\qquad 0\le\alpha\le 1$$

The same equation describes a MIX transition, with $\alpha$ ramping from 0 to 1 over the transition rate. A downstream keyer (DSK) sits after the main mix, so the scorebug stays on screen whatever the director cuts to.

Talkback and IFB

Commentators hear the programme in their headphones through interruptible foldback (IFB): the producer can talk into it. They must receive a mix-minus (programme without their own voice), otherwise they hear themselves delayed and stumble. Gallery talkback is a separate intercom matrix with groups: director to cameras, producer to EVS, sound to the commentary box.

Programme loudness: EBU R128

Broadcasters normalise programmes by perceived loudness, not by peaks. The European Broadcasting Union recommendation R128 (also adopted widely in Africa; the US uses ATSC A/85 at −24 LKFS) sets the target at

$$L_I = -23\ \text{LUFS} \pm 1\ \text{LU (live)},\qquad \text{true peak} \le -1\ \text{dBTP}$$

Loudness is measured with K-weighting (ITU-R BS.1770): a high-shelf boost of about +4 dB above ~1.5 kHz, modelling the head, and a high-pass around 38 Hz, then a mean square over a window:

$$L_K = -0.691 + 10\log_{10}\Big(\sum_i G_i\, z_i\Big)\ \text{LUFS},\qquad z_i=\frac1T\int_0^T y_i^2(t)\,dt$$

with $G_i=1$ for left, right and centre and $1.41$ for surround channels. The meter in the sound desk shows three versions: momentary (400 ms window), short-term (3 s) and integrated (the whole programme, gated: blocks quieter than −70 LUFS, and blocks more than 10 LU below the running average, are ignored so silence does not drag the number down).

Decibels on a fader

A fader applies gain $g$ in decibels; the amplitude multiplies by $10^{g/20}$ and the power by $10^{g/10}$.

$$A_{out}=A_{in}\cdot 10^{g/20}\qquad -6\ \text{dB}\Rightarrow\times 0.50,\quad -20\ \text{dB}\Rightarrow\times 0.10$$

Two uncorrelated sources of equal level add in power, giving +3 dB. That is why opening both crowd microphones raises the crowd by 3 dB, not 6 dB.

$$L_{sum}=10\log_{10}\big(10^{L_1/10}+10^{L_2/10}\big)$$

Football mixing practice

  • Commentary is king. Keep it clearly above the crowd (6 dB or more) and centred. The crowd is the atmosphere bed under it.
  • Ride the crowd. Lift the effects microphones a few dB for a goal and pull back afterwards. The roar is naturally louder, so watch the peaks.
  • Referee microphone. Great for authenticity, dangerous for compliance: dip it the moment tempers flare, before any language reaches air.
  • Clean the lines. Use the high-pass filter (HPF) on speech to remove 50 Hz mains hum and wind rumble; it barely affects the voice, whose fundamental is above ~85 Hz.
  • PFL before you open a fader. Pre-fade listen lets you hear a source in your headphones without putting it on air.
  • Audio-follow-video for replays. Bring up replay audio with the replay, and take it out with the wipe.

1. Compressed match clock

The default match runs each 45-minute half in 300 s of real time.

$$\text{rate}=\frac{45\times60\ \text{s}}{300\ \text{s}} = 9\ \text{match seconds per real second}$$

A 3-minute stoppage-time board therefore lasts $180/9 = 20$ s of real time. On the 6-minute setting (150 s halves) the rate is 18.

2. Transition rates at 25 fps

A MIX rate of 12 frames lasts $12/25 = 0.48$ s; 25 frames is 1.00 s; 50 frames is 2.00 s. Halfway through a 25-frame mix each source contributes $\alpha=0.5$: the picture is $0.5V_A + 0.5V_B$.

3. Replay duration

The EVS operator marks a goal clip from 7 s before the goal to 1.5 s after: 8.5 s of action. Played at 50% speed:

$$t_{air} = \frac{8.5\ \text{s}}{0.5} = 17\ \text{s}$$

The restart after a goal comes about 10–14 s of real time later in this simulation, so a 50% replay of the whole clip can overrun the kick-off. Use 75% (11.3 s) or roll the replay early.

4. Summing crowd microphones

Each crowd microphone contributes −29 LUFS-equivalent. Both open:

$$10\log_{10}\big(2\times 10^{-2.9}\big) = -29 + 3.01 = -26.0\ \text{LUFS}$$

Commentary alone measures −24.5 LUFS while speaking. Total programme while speaking:

$$10\log_{10}\big(10^{-2.45}+10^{-2.60}\big)\approx -22.2\ \text{LUFS}$$

Slightly hot. Pull both crowd faders down 3 dB (crowd −29.0) and the total becomes $10\log_{10}(10^{-2.45}+10^{-2.9}) \approx -23.2$ LUFS: on target, with commentary 4.5 dB above the crowd in loudness terms. The desk’s intelligibility check compares post-fader channel levels, so aim for the 6 dB margin there.

5. Fader law

A fader moved from 0 dB to −10 dB multiplies amplitude by $10^{-10/20}=0.316$ and power by $0.1$. A goal roar 10 dB louder than the crowd bed, with the crowd lifted a further +3 dB, peaks 13 dB above the bed: if the bed sits at −30 dBFS peak the roar reaches −17 dBFS, safe. If you had the crowd far too hot (bed at −12 dBFS) the same roar would reach +1 dBFS and clip.

Challenges

These tick automatically as you work the match. Each one is a real professional habit.

    Suggested exercises

    1. Choose Observe the crew for the first half. Watch the vision mixer desk and write down the order of shots after the first goal or foul. Then direct the second half yourself and compare.
    2. As director, deliberately cut to CAM 7 during live play once. Watch the attack appear to reverse, and read the penalty in the log. Then use CAM 7 only via EVS Angle B on a replay.
    3. As sound supervisor, PFL the ref mic during a heated foul to hear what you are protecting viewers from, without putting it on air.
    4. As producer, try calling replays at 50% after every goal. Do they finish before the restart? What would you change?
    5. Use the same seed in different seats to compare how each member of the crew experiences the same fixture.

    Glossary

    Action axis / 180° line
    Imaginary line along the direction of play; cameras stay on one side of it so left and right remain consistent.
    AUTO / MIX
    An automatic dissolve from programme to preview over a set number of frames.
    CCU
    Camera control unit: powers a camera over fibre and lets a vision engineer adjust iris, black level and colour.
    Cutaway
    A shot away from the main action (crowd, bench) used to cover a stoppage or show reaction.
    DSK
    Downstream keyer: adds graphics after the main mix so they survive cuts.
    EVS
    Brand name commonly used for live replay servers that record all cameras and play clips in slow motion.
    Flip-flop
    Mixer behaviour where, after a cut, the old programme source moves to preview.
    Gantry
    High platform on the main stand, where the wide and tight cameras sit at halfway.
    Hot-punch
    Cutting a source directly on the programme bus without previewing it.
    IFB / mix-minus
    Interruptible foldback: the programme minus the presenter’s own voice, fed to their earpiece, which the producer can talk into.
    ISO camera
    A camera isolated on one player or subject, recorded separately.
    Key and fill
    The transparency (alpha) and colour signals of a graphic.
    Lower third (L3)
    Caption in the lower part of the frame naming a person or event.
    LUFS / LU
    Loudness units relative to full scale; 1 LU difference equals 1 dB.
    Multiviewer
    A single screen split into tiles showing every source, PVW and PGM with tally and labels.
    OB truck
    Outside broadcast vehicle containing gallery, sound, vision control and replay areas.
    PFL
    Pre-fade listen: hear a channel in your headphones without affecting the programme mix.
    PGM / PVW
    Programme (on air) and preview (next shot) buses and monitors.
    Running order
    The timed list of programme items (titles, line-ups, halves, break, wrap). Also called the rundown.
    Scorebug
    Small persistent graphic with score and clock.
    Sting
    A short branded animated transition, used in and out of replays.
    Tally
    Red/green lights on cameras and monitors showing on-air and preview status.
    TX / MCR
    Transmission and master control room: where the broadcaster puts the feed on air and inserts breaks.
    UMD
    Under-monitor display: the label beneath each multiviewer tile.
