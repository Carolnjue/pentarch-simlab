What an element is

An element is defined by its atomic number $Z$, the number of protons in the nucleus. Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons $N$; these are isotopes, written $^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$ where the mass number is

$$A = Z + N$$

A neutral atom has $Z$ electrons. An ion with charge $q$ has $Z - q$ electrons, so $\mathrm{Na^+}$ has 10 electrons and $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ has 18. The standard atomic weight $A_r$ is the abundance-weighted mean of the isotope masses (in units of $\tfrac{1}{12}$ the mass of a $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ atom):

$$A_r = \sum_i x_i\, m_i$$

Shells, subshells and orbitals

Electrons occupy orbitals labelled by quantum numbers: the principal number $n = 1,2,3,\dots$ (the shell), the angular-momentum number $\ell = 0,1,\dots,n-1$ (subshell s, p, d, f), the magnetic number $m_\ell = -\ell,\dots,+\ell$ (one orbital each) and spin $m_s = \pm\tfrac12$. A subshell therefore has $2\ell+1$ orbitals and holds $2(2\ell+1)$ electrons: s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14. A shell holds $2n^2$ electrons.

The Bohr model drawn in the lab is a picture of shells only: electrons are not really little planets on circular tracks; they are standing waves described by orbitals. It is still a useful way to count electrons per shell, and for hydrogen it gives the right energy levels:

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}$$

Filling rules

  • Aufbau (Madelung) rule: subshells fill in order of increasing $n+\ell$, and for equal $n+\ell$ the lower $n$ first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p 7s 5f 6d 7p. The Aufbau filling view animates this diagonal rule.
  • Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons share all four quantum numbers, so each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins.
  • Hund's rule: within a subshell, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. The Orbital boxes view shows this and counts unpaired electrons, which predicts paramagnetism.
The Madelung rule is an approximation. About 20 elements have different ground states, because the energies of $(n-1)$d and $n$s (or $(n-2)$f and $(n-1)$d) are very close. Examples: Cr [Ar] 3d5 4s1, Cu [Ar] 3d10 4s1, Pd [Kr] 4d10, Gd [Xe] 4f7 5d1 6s2, Th [Rn] 6d2 7s2. Select any of them: the lab shows the real configuration and flags the exception.

Why the table has the shape it has

Each period begins a new shell. The width of each block equals the capacity of the subshell being filled: s-block 2 columns, p-block 6, d-block 10, f-block 14. Elements in the same group have the same outer (valence) configuration, which is why they react alike.

Effective nuclear charge and the trends

An outer electron is attracted by $Z$ protons but repelled by the other electrons, which partly shield it. Its effective nuclear charge is

$$Z_\text{eff} = Z - S$$

where $S$ is a shielding constant (Slater's rules give a quick estimate). Across a period $Z$ rises by one each step while shielding by electrons in the same shell is poor, so $Z_\text{eff}$ climbs. Down a group a new shell is added further from the nucleus. These two facts explain the main trends:

The first ionisation energy is the energy for $\mathrm{X(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{X^+(g)} + e^-$, per mole. Its small dips at boron (removing a higher-energy 2p electron) and oxygen (removing a paired 2p electron, which feels extra repulsion) are clear evidence for subshells.

Electronegativity measures an atom's pull on shared bonding electrons. Pauling defined differences from bond energies (in eV):

$$\chi_A - \chi_B = \sqrt{E_d(AB) - \tfrac12\left[E_d(AA) + E_d(BB)\right]}$$

with fluorine fixed at 3.98. Noble gases He, Ne and Ar form no stable compounds, so they have no Pauling value; they appear grey in the heatmap.

About the data

Atomic weights: IUPAC conventional/abridged values. Electronegativity: Pauling scale. Radius: single-bond covalent radii (Cordero et al., 2008; low-spin values for Mn, Fe, Co). Ionisation energies: NIST. Density at about 20 °C (gases at 0 °C, 1 atm; carbon as graphite, phosphorus as white P, tin as white Sn). Melting and boiling points at 1 atm; carbon and arsenic sublime. Values that have not been measured are left blank rather than estimated. Configurations for Z ≥ 104 are predictions.

1. Relative atomic mass of chlorine

Chlorine is 75.76 % $^{35}\mathrm{Cl}$ (34.969) and 24.24 % $^{37}\mathrm{Cl}$ (36.966).

$$A_r = 0.7576(34.969) + 0.2424(36.966) = 26.492 + 8.961 = 35.45$$

This matches the value 35.45 shown when you select Cl. Note that no single chlorine atom has mass 35.45: it is an average.

2. Configuration of iron and the Fe3+ ion

Fe has $Z=26$. Following the Aufbau order: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d6, written [Ar] 3d6 4s2 (2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 26).

When a transition metal ionises, the 4s electrons leave first (once 3d is occupied it lies lower in energy than 4s). Fe2+ = [Ar] 3d6; Fe3+ = [Ar] 3d5, a half-filled d subshell with 5 unpaired electrons, which is why Fe3+ salts are strongly paramagnetic. Open Fe in the Orbital boxes view to see the 4 unpaired electrons of the neutral atom.

3. Effective nuclear charge with Slater's rules

Sodium, 3s electron. Groupings: (1s)(2s,2p)(3s). Electrons in shell $n-1$ shield 0.85 each, shells $n-2$ and below 1.00 each.

$$S = 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 8.80,\qquad Z_\text{eff} = 11 - 8.80 = 2.20$$

Chlorine, 3p electron. Other electrons in (3s,3p) shield 0.35 each: there are 6 of them.

$$S = 6(0.35) + 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 10.90,\qquad Z_\text{eff} = 17 - 10.90 = 6.10$$

The outer electron of Cl feels nearly three times the pull felt by that of Na, so Cl is smaller (102 pm vs 166 pm), harder to ionise (1251 vs 496 kJ/mol) and more electronegative (3.16 vs 0.93). Compare the two in the explorer.

4. Ionisation energy of hydrogen from the Bohr model

Removing the electron from $n=1$ to $n=\infty$ needs $\Delta E = 0 - (-13.6\ \text{eV}) = 13.6$ eV per atom. Per mole:

$$13.598\ \text{eV} \times 96.485\ \tfrac{\text{kJ mol}^{-1}}{\text{eV}} = 1312\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$$

This is exactly the tabulated first ionisation energy of H (1312.0 kJ/mol).

5. Counting atoms in a gold ring

A 10.0 g pure gold ring: $n = m/M = 10.0 / 196.97 = 0.0508$ mol, so the number of atoms is $0.0508 \times 6.022\times10^{23} = 3.06\times10^{22}$. Its volume is $m/\rho = 10.0/19.3 = 0.518\ \text{cm}^3$.
