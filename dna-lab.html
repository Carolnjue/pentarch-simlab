    Key facts to remember

    • Sickle cell: HBB c.20A>T turns codon GAG (Glu) into GTG (Val): Glu6Val (HGVS p.Glu7Val). Autosomal recessive: AS × AS gives a 1 in 4 chance of SS in each pregnancy.
    • Kenya's Ministry of Health estimates about 14 000 babies are born with sickle cell disease each year, with the highest burden around Lake Victoria, in Western and on the Coast. A 2023 policy introduces infant screening.
    • Sickle-cell trait (HbAS) gave about 90 % protection against severe falciparum malaria in children on the Kenyan coast (Williams et al., 2005): heterozygote advantage.
    • Laboratory: sickling and solubility tests screen but cannot tell AS from SS. Alkaline Hb electrophoresis (A runs fastest to the anode, then F, then S; C runs with A2) or HPLC confirms. PCR-RFLP: the mutation destroys a DdeI/MstII site (CCTGAGG → CCTGTGG).
    • HIV copies RNA into DNA with reverse transcriptase. NRTIs (tenofovir, lamivudine) lack a 3′-OH and terminate the chain; dolutegravir blocks integrase. Kenya 2022: viral load < 50 copies/mL = LDL, < 200 = suppressed, ≥ 1000 after 3 months of ART = suspected treatment failure.
    • GeneXpert MTB/RIF is real-time PCR in under 2 hours: five molecular beacons cover the 81-bp core of rpoB, where over 95 % of rifampicin-resistance mutations lie.
    • Antibiotics on the central dogma: fluoroquinolones (DNA gyrase), rifampicin (RNA polymerase), aminoglycosides and tetracyclines (30S), macrolides and chloramphenicol (50S).

    Sources: Kenya MoH (infant SCD screening policy, 2023); Williams et al., J Infect Dis 2005; Kenya HIV Prevention and Treatment Guidelines 2022; European Respiratory Journal review of Xpert MTB/RIF; standard haematology and pharmacology texts.     1. DNA: structure and base pairing

    DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands. Each nucleotide has a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate and one of four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) or cytosine (C). The sugar–phosphate backbone is joined by 3′→5′ phosphodiester bonds, so each strand has a 5′ end (free phosphate) and a 3′ end (free OH). Bases pair by hydrogen bonds: A with T (2 H-bonds) and G with C (3 H-bonds). Chargaff’s rule follows directly: in double-stranded DNA, $[A]=[T]$ and $[G]=[C]$.

    B-DNA has about 10.5 base pairs per helical turn, a rise of 0.34 nm per base pair (so one turn ≈ 3.4–3.6 nm) and a diameter of about 2 nm. Because the glycosidic bonds of a base pair are not diametrically opposite, the helix has a wide major groove and a narrow minor groove, which proteins read to recognise sequences.

    $$L \approx 0.34\,\text{nm} \times N_{bp},\qquad \text{turns} \approx \frac{N_{bp}}{10.5},\qquad T_m \approx 81.5 + 16.6\log_{10}[\text{Na}^+] + 0.41(\%GC) - \frac{675}{N}\;\;(^{\circ}\text{C})$$

    Here $L$ is the length of the double helix, $N_\text{bp}$ the number of base pairs, %GC the percentage of G–C pairs, and $T_m$ the melting temperature, at which half the DNA has separated into single strands. The melting-temperature formula is an empirical approximation for long DNA; [Na+] is the sodium concentration in mol/L, and the lab assumes 50 mM Na+, a typical PCR buffer, so the salt term is about −21.6 °C; more G–C pairs mean more hydrogen bonds and stronger base stacking, so higher $T_m$.

    2. Replication is semi-conservative

    Each daughter duplex keeps one parental strand (Meselson–Stahl, 1958). At the replication fork, topoisomerase relieves the supercoiling ahead, helicase unwinds the strands, single-strand binding proteins stop them re-annealing, and primase lays short RNA primers because DNA polymerases can only extend an existing 3′-OH. Polymerases synthesise only 5′→3′, so one strand (the leading strand) is made continuously towards the fork, while the other (the lagging strand) is made in short Okazaki fragments (≈1000–2000 nt in bacteria, ≈100–200 nt in eukaryotes) away from it. In E. coli, DNA polymerase I removes the RNA primers and fills the gaps; in eukaryotes RNase H and FEN1 remove them and polymerase δ fills in. DNA ligase seals the final nicks.

    3. Transcription

    RNA polymerase II binds the promoter with general transcription factors. TFIID’s TATA-binding protein recognises the TATA box (consensus TATAAA, about 25–30 bp upstream of the transcription start site, +1). The polymerase opens a ~12–14 bp bubble, reads the template (antisense) strand 3′→5′ and joins ribonucleotides 5′→3′. The RNA therefore has the same sequence as the coding (sense) strand, with uracil in place of thymine.

    $$\text{template }3'\text{-TAC GGA-}5' \;\longrightarrow\; \text{mRNA }5'\text{-AUG CCU-}3' \;=\; \text{coding }5'\text{-ATG CCT-}3'\ (T\to U)$$

    In eukaryotes, the AAUAAA poly(A) signal near the 3′ end directs cleavage of the transcript 10–30 nt downstream; the polymerase continues for a while and is then released (the “torpedo” model).

    4. Processing and splicing

    The primary transcript (pre-mRNA) is processed in the nucleus:

    • 5′ cap: a 7-methylguanosine joined by an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate bridge. It protects the mRNA from 5′ exonucleases and recruits the ribosome (via eIF4E).
    • Splicing: introns are removed and exons joined by the spliceosome (snRNPs U1, U2, U4, U5, U6 plus ~150 proteins). Almost all introns obey the GU–AG rule: they start with GU at the 5′ splice site and end with AG at the 3′ splice site, with a branch-point A about 18–40 nt upstream of the 3′ end followed by a polypyrimidine tract.
    • 3′ cleavage and polyadenylation: poly(A) polymerase adds ~200 adenines (the length varies by species and transcript).

    Splicing is two transesterifications. Step 1: the 2′-OH of the branch-point A attacks the phosphate at the 5′ splice site, releasing exon 1 and forming a lariat with a 2′–5′ phosphodiester bond. Step 2: the free 3′-OH of exon 1 attacks the 3′ splice site, joining the exons and releasing the lariat intron, which is debranched and degraded.

    Because the genetic code is read in triplets, cutting even one nucleotide off target shifts the reading frame for everything downstream. This is why splice sites must be precise, and why many disease mutations (including several β-thalassaemias) act on splicing.

    Alternative splicing lets one gene make several proteins (isoforms) by including or skipping exons, choosing alternative 5′ or 3′ splice sites, or retaining introns. Over 90% of human multi-exon genes are alternatively spliced. An exon whose length is a multiple of 3 can be skipped “in frame”; skipping an exon of $3k+1$ or $3k+2$ nt shifts the frame.

    5. Translation

    The small ribosomal subunit binds the cap and scans 5′→3′ to the first AUG in a good context (Kozak sequence, e.g. GCCACCAUGG). The initiator Met-tRNA sits in the P site; the large subunit joins. Each elongation cycle: (1) an aminoacyl-tRNA whose anticodon pairs with the codon enters the A site; (2) the ribosome (a ribozyme: the peptidyl transferase is rRNA) moves the growing chain onto the A-site amino acid, forming a peptide bond; (3) the ribosome translocates one codon, moving tRNAs from A→P and P→E (exit). A stop codon (UAA, UAG, UGA) is recognised by a release factor, and the polypeptide is freed.

    $$\text{codons} = 4^3 = 64:\quad 61\ \text{sense codons} \to 20\ \text{amino acids},\quad 3\ \text{stop codons}$$
    $$M_r(\text{protein}) \approx \sum_i m_{\text{residue},i} + 18.02\ \text{Da}\qquad(\text{average residue} \approx 110\ \text{Da})$$

    The code is degenerate (several codons per amino acid, usually differing at the third base, which also allows wobble pairing), unambiguous (each codon means one thing) and nearly universal.

    Sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a single substitution in HBB: c.20A>T changes codon GAG (Glu) to GTG (Val). The β-globin residue is traditionally called Glu6Val because numbering starts after the initiator methionine (HGVS: p.Glu7Val).

    7. CRISPR-Cas9

    Cas9 from Streptococcus pyogenes is guided by a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) whose 20-nt spacer pairs with the DNA strand opposite the protospacer. Cas9 only binds where the protospacer is immediately followed by a PAM, 5′-NGG-3′. Its HNH and RuvC nuclease domains cut both strands 3 bp upstream (5′) of the PAM, leaving a blunt double-strand break. Cells repair it by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ: error-prone, small insertions or deletions, often a frameshift that knocks the gene out) or, if a donor template is supplied and the cell is dividing, by homology-directed repair (HDR: precise edits copied from the template).

    Model simplifications: sequences are shown as straight strands; nucleotide positions of enzymes and the number of hydrogen bonds per pair are exact, but distances and rates on the stage are not to scale. Real introns are usually much longer (median ≈1.5 kb in humans); teaching genes use short introns so you can see every base.

    Example 1: from template strand to protein

    Template strand: 3′-TAC CGA TTT GTC ACT-5′

    1. Pair each base (A→U, T→A, G→C, C→G) and keep the direction antiparallel: mRNA 5′-AUG GCU AAA CAG UGA-3′.
    2. The coding strand is identical with T: 5′-ATG GCT AAA CAG TGA-3′.
    3. Translate from AUG in triplets: AUG Met, GCU Ala, AAA Lys, CAG Gln, UGA stop.
    4. Protein: Met-Ala-Lys-Gln (MAKQ), 4 residues. Type ATGGCTAAACAGTGA into “Build your own gene” on Station 1 and run it through Stations 2 and 4 to check.

    Example 2: splice sites in the Message gene

    The Message teaching gene has four exons of 63, 15, 13 and 78 nt. Exon 1 holds a 39-nt 5′ UTR, so the coding part of exon 1 is $63-39=24$ nt (8 codons: M S P L I C E D).

    1. Exon 2 is 15 nt $=5$ codons, a multiple of 3. Skipping it removes R I G H T and keeps the frame: MSPLICED·THEN·TRANSLATED (22 aa).
    2. Exon 3 is 13 nt $=3\times4+1$. Skipping it shifts the reading frame of exon 4 by one nucleotide: CGA GAG CCA ATT CCC TAG, giving MSPLICEDRIGHTREPIP then stop (18 aa).
    3. Correct splicing gives MSPLICEDRIGHTTHENTRANSLATED: 27 residues and a stop codon, so the CDS is $28\times3=84$ nt.

    Example 3: sickle-cell haemoglobin

    HBB coding sequence starts ATG GTG CAT CTG ACT CCT GAG GAG = M V H L T P E E.

    1. The 7th codon (6th after Met) is GAG = Glu. Changing nucleotide 20 (A→T) gives GTG = Val.
    2. Glu is acidic (charged, hydrophilic); Val is hydrophobic. On the surface of deoxy-haemoglobin, Val6 sticks into a hydrophobic pocket on a neighbouring molecule, so HbS polymerises into fibres that sickle the red cell.
    3. Only 1 of 444 coding nucleotides changed (0.23%), and only 1 of 146 residues of the mature chain.

    Example 4: frameshift β-thalassaemia (codons 41/42, −CTTT)

    1. Codons 41–44 (traditional numbering) are TTC TTT GAG TCC = F F E S.
    2. Deleting 4 nt (CTTT) leaves TTG AGT CCT TTG GGG ATC TGT CCA CTC CTG ATG CTG TTA TGG GCA ACC CTA AGG TGA…
    3. 4 is not a multiple of 3, so every downstream codon is misread; a new UGA stop appears 18 codons later. Try it in the Mutation lab (preset “codons 41/42”).

    Example 5: where does Cas9 cut?

    Top strand: 5′-…GCC GTT ACT GCC CTG TGG GGC AAG G…-3′. Candidate PAMs (NGG) on this strand include TGG and GGG; GGC is not a PAM. (A CCN on the top strand, such as the CCG in GCC GTT, is an NGG PAM on the bottom strand.)

    1. Choose PAM = TGG (codon 16 counting the initiator Met; Trp15 in traditional numbering). The 20 nt immediately 5′ of it are the protospacer; the guide RNA has the same sequence (with U) and pairs with the bottom strand.
    2. Cas9 cuts between protospacer nucleotides 17 and 18, i.e. 3 bp upstream of the PAM, on both strands: a blunt break.
    3. A 1-nt insertion by NHEJ at that point shifts the frame; HDR with a donor can instead write in a precise change.

    Example 6: protein mass

    The mature β-globin chain has 146 residues (the initiator Met is removed after translation). Sum of residue masses ≈ 15 849 Da; add 18.02 for the terminal H and OH: $M_r \approx 15\,867$ Da, close to the measured 15 867 Da. The lab’s readout includes Met (147 residues ≈ 15.998 kDa).

    Sickle cell in East Africa

    The HbS allele is common where malaria has been common, because carriers (HbAS) are partly protected against severe Plasmodium falciparum malaria: heterozygote advantage. In areas of high malaria transmission, sickle-cell trait (HbAS) can be carried by 15–20% or more of people. Newborn screening, hydroxyurea (which raises fetal haemoglobin, HbF) and penicillin prophylaxis greatly improve survival.

    Gene editing reaches the clinic

    In 2023 the UK and US approved exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy), the first CRISPR-Cas9 medicine, for sickle-cell disease and transfusion-dependent β-thalassaemia. It does not repair HBB: it uses NHEJ to disrupt an erythroid enhancer of BCL11A, a repressor of fetal γ-globin, so patients’ red cells make HbF again. Research groups are also testing HDR and base editing to correct the HbS mutation directly.

    Splicing as a drug target

    Spinal muscular atrophy is treated with nusinersen, an antisense oligonucleotide that blocks a splicing silencer in SMN2 intron 7 so that exon 7 is included and a working SMN protein is made. Risdiplam, a small molecule taken by mouth, does the same job. About 10–15% of disease-causing point mutations act on splicing.

    Insulin made by microbes

    Human insulin was the first recombinant medicine (1982). Bacteria cannot splice introns, so manufacturers express an intron-free coding sequence. One yeast process makes a single-chain precursor, B(1–29)–Ala–Ala–Lys–A(1–21), which is cleaved and completed enzymatically (adding Thr B30) to give human insulin; the same precursor with Asp in place of Pro at B28 is the route to insulin aspart. The Mini-insulin teaching gene in this lab encodes the human precursor.

    Diagnosing sickle cell in the laboratory

    Screening tests (sodium metabisulphite sickling, solubility) show that HbS is present, but both AS and SS are positive. Confirmation separates the haemoglobins by charge. At alkaline pH (≈ 8.4–8.6) on cellulose acetate, haemoglobins are negatively charged and run from the cathode towards the anode; HbS has lost a negative glutamate, so it runs behind HbA. Newborns are mostly HbF, so screening at birth reads F, A and S together.
