Energy profile

Collision theory

For molecules to react they must collide, with enough energy along the line of their centres to overcome the activation barrier $E_a$, and in the right orientation. The rate constant therefore contains three factors:

$$k = Z\,p\,e^{-E_a/RT}$$

$Z$ is the collision frequency factor (it grows with concentration, molecular size and speed), $p$ is the steric (orientation) factor and $e^{-E_a/RT}$ is the fraction of collisions with energy above $E_a$. In the chamber, a reaction happens exactly when a collision meets all three conditions. The "Collision energies" view shows the measured distribution, which is exponential, $f(E) = \frac{1}{RT}e^{-E/RT}$, so the shaded fraction above $E_a$ is $e^{-E_a/RT}$.

Activation energy, enthalpy and catalysts

On a reaction-coordinate diagram the reverse barrier is $E_{a,r} = E_{a,f} - \Delta H$. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower barrier. It lowers both barriers by the same amount, so it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally: equilibrium is reached sooner, but the position of equilibrium and $K$ do not change.

Dynamic equilibrium

As A and B are used up, the forward rate falls; as C and D accumulate, the reverse rate rises. At equilibrium the rates are equal and concentrations stop changing, although particles keep reacting in both directions. For the elementary step A + B ⇌ C + D:

$$\text{rate}_f = k_f[\mathrm A][\mathrm B],\quad \text{rate}_r = k_r[\mathrm C][\mathrm D]\quad\Rightarrow\quad K = \frac{[\mathrm C][\mathrm D]}{[\mathrm A][\mathrm B]} = \frac{k_f}{k_r}$$

The reaction quotient $Q$ has the same form but uses the current concentrations. If $Q < K$ the net reaction goes forward; if $Q > K$ it goes backward. In this chamber all particles have the same mass and size, so $K = e^{-\Delta H/RT}$ (the entropy change is zero by construction); the van ’t Hoff equation then says $\ln K$ falls with $T$ for an exothermic reaction and rises for an endothermic one.

Le Chatelier's principle

  • Concentration: adding a reactant or removing a product makes $Q < K$; the system shifts right until $Q = K$ again. $K$ itself is unchanged.
  • Temperature: the only stress that changes $K$. Heating favours the endothermic direction.
  • Pressure / volume: only matters if the number of gas molecules changes. Here $\Delta n = 0$, so compressing changes every concentration by the same factor and $Q$ is unchanged: no shift, although rates increase. For the Haber process ($\Delta n = -2$) compression shifts the equilibrium to the right.
  • Catalyst: no shift; equilibrium is just reached sooner.
To make reactions visible within seconds, the chamber uses scaled activation energies of a few kJ mol⁻¹ (real values are typically 40–200 kJ mol⁻¹) and two-dimensional motion. The Rate laws, Arrhenius and Haber tabs use realistic values.

The Arrhenius equation

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{T}$$

A plot of $\ln k$ against $1/T$ is a straight line with gradient $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\ln A$. Two measurements are enough to find $E_a$: $\ln(k_2/k_1) = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)$.

The initial-rates method

For rate $= k[\mathrm A]^m[\mathrm B]^n$, compare experiments in which only one concentration changes. If doubling [A] (with [B] fixed) doubles the rate, $m = 1$; if it quadruples the rate, $m = 2$; if nothing changes, $m = 0$. In general $m = \log(\text{rate ratio})/\log(\text{concentration ratio})$. Then substitute any row to find $k$.

Industrial example: the Haber process

$$\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3\,H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\,NH_3(g)}\qquad \Delta H^\circ = -92\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$$

Four moles of gas become two and the reaction is exothermic. Le Chatelier says high pressure and low temperature give the highest equilibrium yield, but at low temperature the reaction (even over an iron catalyst) is far too slow, and very high pressures are expensive and dangerous. Plants therefore compromise at about 400–450 °C and 150–250 atm and recycle unreacted gas, converting roughly 15–20 % of it per pass.

The rate and per-pass yield come from an illustrative model: the approach to equilibrium follows first-order kinetics with $E_a = 100\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, rate ∝ √P, scaled so that the gas reaches 86 % of its equilibrium yield in one pass at 450 °C and 200 atm.

Example 1: Equilibrium in the chamber

Default chamber: ΔH = −5.0 kJ mol⁻¹, T = 350 K. $RT = 8.314\times10^{-3}\times350 = 2.910$ kJ mol⁻¹, so $K = e^{-\Delta H/RT} = e^{5.0/2.910} = $ 5.58. Starting with equal amounts $a$ of A and B, $x^2/(a-x)^2 = 5.58$ gives $x/(a-x) = 2.36$, so $x = 0.703a$: about 70 % of A converts. With 70 A and 70 B you should see roughly 49 C and 49 D at equilibrium.

Example 2: Fraction of successful collisions

With the default $E_a = 6.0$ kJ mol⁻¹ at 350 K, the fraction of collisions energetic enough is $e^{-6.0/2.910} = $ 0.127. With the catalyst ($E_a = 3.6$ kJ mol⁻¹) it is $e^{-3.6/2.910} = 0.290$, 2.3 times as many. Heating to 450 K without a catalyst gives $e^{-6.0/3.741} = 0.201$. Of the energetic collisions, only about one in three has the right orientation ($p = 1/3$). Compare with the "Collision energies" view.

Example 3: First-order half-life

The decomposition of N₂O₅ is first order with $k = 6.2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 45 °C. $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k = 0.693/6.2\times10^{-4} = $ 1120 s (18.6 min). After three half-lives, $1/8$ remains: $[\mathrm A] = 0.125[\mathrm A]_0$.

Example 4: Activation energy from two rate constants

$k_1 = 2.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 300 K and $k_2 = 1.6\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 330 K. $E_a = \dfrac{R\ln(k_2/k_1)}{1/T_1 - 1/T_2} = \dfrac{8.314\times\ln 8}{3.333\times10^{-3} - 3.030\times10^{-3}} = \dfrac{17.29}{3.030\times10^{-4}} = $ 57.1 kJ mol⁻¹.

Example 6: Haber equilibrium at 450 °C and 200 atm

$K_p(723\ \mathrm K) = 4.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{atm^{-2}}$. With a 1 : 3 feed, let $x$ mol of N₂ react: $y_{\mathrm{NH_3}} = 2x/(4-2x)$ and $K_p = \dfrac{y_{\mathrm{NH_3}}^2}{y_{\mathrm{N_2}}\,y_{\mathrm{H_2}}^3\,P^2}$. Solving numerically gives $x = 0.40$ and 24.9 % NH₃ at equilibrium (the tab shows the same value). At 400 °C it would be 35 %, but the rate would be about four times slower.
