Energy profile

Collision theory

For molecules to react they must collide, with enough energy along the line of their centres (the head-on part of the impact; a glancing blow does not count) to overcome the activation barrier $E_a$, and in the right orientation. The rate constant therefore contains three factors:

$$k = Z\,p\,e^{-E_a/RT}$$

$Z$ is the collision frequency factor (it grows with concentration, molecular size and speed), $p$ is the steric (orientation) factor and $e^{-E_a/RT}$ is the fraction of collisions with energy above $E_a$. In the chamber, a reaction happens exactly when a collision meets all three conditions. The "Collision energies" view shows the measured distribution, which is exponential, $f(E) = \frac{1}{RT}e^{-E/RT}$, so the shaded fraction above $E_a$ is $e^{-E_a/RT}$. Textbook diagrams usually show the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve of molecular energies, which rises to a peak before its long tail. This view plots the energies of collisions in a two-dimensional chamber, which has no peak. The conclusion is the same: raising $T$ or lowering $E_a$ increases the area beyond $E_a$.

Activation energy, enthalpy and catalysts

A reaction-coordinate diagram (the Energy profile in the lab) plots energy as the reactants turn into products. $\Delta H$ is the energy difference between products and reactants: negative means exothermic (heat released), positive means endothermic (heat absorbed). The reverse barrier is $E_{a,r} = E_{a,f} - \Delta H$. A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower barrier. It lowers both barriers by the same amount, so it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally: equilibrium is reached sooner, but the position of equilibrium and $K$ do not change.

Dynamic equilibrium

As A and B are used up, the forward rate falls; as C and D accumulate, the reverse rate rises. At equilibrium the rates are equal and concentrations stop changing, although particles keep reacting in both directions. For the elementary step A + B ⇌ C + D (a single-collision step, so its rate law can be read straight from the equation):

$$\text{rate}_f = k_f[\mathrm A][\mathrm B],\quad \text{rate}_r = k_r[\mathrm C][\mathrm D]\quad\Rightarrow\quad K = \frac{[\mathrm C][\mathrm D]}{[\mathrm A][\mathrm B]} = \frac{k_f}{k_r}$$

The reaction quotient $Q$ has the same form but uses the current concentrations. If $Q < K$ the net reaction goes forward; if $Q > K$ it goes backward. In this chamber all particles have the same mass and size, so $K = e^{-\Delta H/RT}$ (the entropy change is zero by construction); the van ’t Hoff equation then says $\ln K$ falls with $T$ for an exothermic reaction and rises for an endothermic one.

Le Chatelier's principle

  • Concentration: adding a reactant or removing a product makes $Q < K$; the system shifts right until $Q = K$ again. $K$ itself is unchanged.
  • Temperature: the only stress that changes $K$. Heating favours the endothermic direction.
  • Pressure / volume: only matters if the number of gas molecules changes. Here $\Delta n = 0$, so compressing changes every concentration by the same factor and $Q$ is unchanged: no shift, although rates increase. For the Haber process ($\Delta n = -2$) compression shifts the equilibrium to the right.
  • Catalyst: no shift; equilibrium is just reached sooner.
Model scale. To make reactions visible within seconds, every energy in the chamber (Eₐ, ΔH and the collision energies) is in scaled model kJ mol⁻¹, about ten times smaller than real values (real activation energies are typically 40–200 kJ mol⁻¹), and motion is two-dimensional. The physics depends only on the ratio $E_a/RT$, which is about 2 in the chamber but 15–70 for real reactions at room temperature, which is why real reactions are far slower: only about 1 collision in 10⁹ succeeds when $E_a/RT = 20$. The Rate laws, Arrhenius and Haber tabs use realistic values.

The Arrhenius equation

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{T}$$

A plot of $\ln k$ against $1/T$ is a straight line with gradient $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\ln A$. Two measurements are enough to find $E_a$: $\ln(k_2/k_1) = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)$.

The initial-rates method

For rate $= k[\mathrm A]^m[\mathrm B]^n$, compare experiments in which only one concentration changes. If doubling [A] (with [B] fixed) doubles the rate, $m = 1$; if it quadruples the rate, $m = 2$; if nothing changes, $m = 0$. In general $m = \log(\text{rate ratio})/\log(\text{concentration ratio})$. Then substitute any row to find $k$.

Orders must be found by experiment. They need not match the coefficients in the balanced equation: the 2 in 2A says nothing about $m$. Only for a single elementary step can the rate law be read from the equation. In a multi-step mechanism, the slowest step (the rate-determining step) sets the rate law.

Industrial example: the Haber process

$$\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3\,H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\,NH_3(g)}\qquad \Delta H^\circ = -92\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$$

Four moles of gas become two and the reaction is exothermic. Le Chatelier says high pressure and low temperature give the highest equilibrium yield, but at low temperature the reaction (even over an iron catalyst) is far too slow, and very high pressures are expensive and dangerous. Plants therefore compromise at about 400–450 °C and 150–250 atm and recycle unreacted gas, converting roughly 15–25 % of it per pass.

Equilibrium yields use $K_p(T)$ from the Gillespie–Beattie correlation with a 1 : 3 N₂ : H₂ feed and ideal-gas behaviour (at 200 atm real gases give a few per cent more NH₃). The rate and per-pass yield come from an illustrative model: the approach to equilibrium follows first-order kinetics with $E_a = 100\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, rate ∝ √P, scaled so that the gas reaches 86 % of its equilibrium yield in one pass at 450 °C and 200 atm.

Example 1: Equilibrium in the chamber (model scale)

Default chamber: ΔH = −5.0 model kJ mol⁻¹, T = 350 K. $RT = 8.314\times10^{-3}\times350 = 2.910$ kJ mol⁻¹, so $K = e^{-\Delta H/RT} = e^{5.0/2.910} = $ 5.58. Starting with equal amounts $a$ of A and B, $x^2/(a-x)^2 = 5.58$ gives $x/(a-x) = 2.36$, so $x = 0.703a$: about 70 % of A converts. With 70 A and 70 B you should see roughly 49 C and 49 D at equilibrium.

Example 2: Fraction of successful collisions (model scale)

With the default $E_a = 6.0$ model kJ mol⁻¹ at 350 K, the fraction of collisions energetic enough is $e^{-6.0/2.910} = $ 0.127. With the catalyst ($E_a = 3.6$ model kJ mol⁻¹) it is $e^{-3.6/2.910} = 0.290$, 2.3 times as many. Heating to 450 K without a catalyst gives $e^{-6.0/3.741} = 0.201$. Of the energetic collisions, only about one in three has the right orientation ($p = 1/3$). Compare with the "Collision energies" view. A realistic barrier of 60 kJ mol⁻¹ at the same temperature gives $e^{-60/2.910} = 1.1\times10^{-9}$: only about one collision in a billion.

Example 3: First-order half-life

The decomposition of N₂O₅ is first order with $k = 6.2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 45 °C. $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k = 0.693/6.2\times10^{-4} = $ 1120 s (18.6 min). After three half-lives, $1/8$ remains: $[\mathrm A] = 0.125[\mathrm A]_0$.

Example 4: Activation energy from two rate constants

$k_1 = 2.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 300 K and $k_2 = 1.6\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ at 330 K. $E_a = \dfrac{R\ln(k_2/k_1)}{1/T_1 - 1/T_2} = \dfrac{8.314\times\ln 8}{3.333\times10^{-3} - 3.030\times10^{-3}} = \dfrac{17.29}{3.030\times10^{-4}} = $ 57.1 kJ mol⁻¹.

Example 6: Haber equilibrium at 450 °C and 200 atm

$K_p(723\ \mathrm K) = 4.6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{atm^{-2}}$. With a 1 : 3 feed, let $x$ mol of N₂ react: $y_{\mathrm{NH_3}} = 2x/(4-2x)$ and $K_p = \dfrac{y_{\mathrm{NH_3}}^2}{y_{\mathrm{N_2}}\,y_{\mathrm{H_2}}^3\,P^2}$. Solving numerically gives $x = 0.40$ and 24.9 % NH₃ at equilibrium (the tab shows the same value). At 400 °C it would be 35 %, but with $E_a$ = 100 kJ mol⁻¹ the rate would be about 3.4 times slower ($e^{-(E_a/R)(1/673 - 1/723)} = 0.29$).

    1. Rates meet. Reset and watch the forward and reverse rates. Note the time at which they become equal and compare with when the concentration curves level off.
    2. Measure K. At equilibrium, read [A], [B], [C], [D] and calculate $K$. Compare with $e^{-\Delta H/RT}$. Repeat at 250 K and 600 K.
    3. Catalyst race. Reset with and without the catalyst at the same temperature. Which reaches equilibrium first? Are the final concentrations different?
    4. Turn orientation off. How much faster does equilibrium arrive? The steric factor in this model is 1/3.
    5. Insulated exothermic reaction. Tick "insulated" and reset the exothermic preset. Why does the temperature climb, and why does the climb slow down?
