Who controls what

A flight passes through several air traffic control units, each responsible for a block of airspace. Control is handed from one to the next by a handoff (radar transfer plus a frequency change).

  • Ground (GND): aircraft and vehicles on taxiways and aprons.
  • Tower (TWR, aerodrome control): the runways and the control zone. Issues line-up, take-off and landing clearances.
  • Approach (APP, terminal radar): sequences arrivals onto final approach and climbs departures out. Uses radar vectors (headings, altitudes, speeds).
  • Area / en route (ACC): cruise traffic over large regions. In Kenya, Nairobi ACC covers the Nairobi Flight Information Region.

In this simulator you work a combined approach and tower position, as small units often do at quiet times: you vector arrivals, clear them to land, clear departures for take-off and hand them to Nairobi Control.

The airport is HKJK-style: a single runway 06/24, about 4.1 km long, at an elevation of about 5,330 ft. Fixes, procedures and traffic are fictional, simplified for learning, and must never be used for real navigation.

Standard phraseology (ICAO Doc 9432 / Annex 10)

Radio calls are short, unambiguous and always read back. Numbers are spoken digit by digit, except whole hundreds and thousands. The callsign comes first from the controller and last in the pilot's readback.

"Take-off" is used only for the take-off clearance itself; at other times controllers say "departure". This rule exists because of the 1977 Tenerife disaster, where an ambiguous "OK… at take-off" contributed to the deadliest accident in aviation history.

The ICAO phonetic alphabet

Digits: 3 is "tree", 5 is "fife", 9 is "niner", 1,000 is "tousand", and the decimal point is "decimal".

Separation minima used here

Two aircraft under radar control must keep at least one of these apart:

  • Vertical: 1,000 ft (below FL290, and above it in RVSM airspace).
  • Horizontal (radar): 3 NM within a terminal area with suitable radar (5 NM is common en route).

A loss of separation occurs when both are infringed at once:

$$d_h < 3\ \text{NM} \quad\text{and}\quad |\Delta h| < 1000\ \text{ft}$$

Conflict prediction (STCA)

Short Term Conflict Alert extrapolates each track. For two aircraft with relative position $\mathbf r$ and relative velocity $\mathbf v$, the time and distance of closest approach are:

$$t_{\text{CPA}} = -\frac{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v}{|\mathbf v|^2}, \qquad d_{\text{CPA}} = \big|\mathbf r + \mathbf v\,t_{\text{CPA}}\big|$$

The simulator alerts (amber) when the predicted horizontal distance falls below 3 NM with less than 1,000 ft vertically within the next 90 s, allowing for climbs and descents, and shows a red STCA when separation is actually lost.

The Instrument Landing System

An ILS gives a precise approach path with two radio beams: the localiser (lateral guidance along the runway centreline, VHF 108–112 MHz) and the glide path (vertical guidance, UHF 329–335 MHz, normally 3°). Marker beacons or DME provide range.

Glide path height

$$h_{\text{AGL}} = d \cdot 6076\ \text{ft/NM} \cdot \tan 3^\circ \approx 318\,d\ \text{ft}\quad(d \text{ in NM})$$

The airport is 5,330 ft above sea level, so an aircraft levelled at 8,000 ft QNH (2,670 ft above the threshold) meets the glide path at $d = 2670/318 \approx 8.4$ NM. That is the final approach fix (FAF) drawn on the scope.

Vectoring to the localiser

  • Give an intercept heading of 30° or less to the final approach course (ICAO allows up to 45°); larger angles cause overshoots.
  • Intercept at least 2 NM before the glide path intercept, level at or below the glide path: aircraft capture the glide slope from below.
  • Clear the aircraft for the ILS with the intercept heading: "turn right heading 030, cleared ILS approach runway 06".
  • Slow arrivals to 180–160 kt by 8–10 NM so the sequence is stable, then let them fly their own approach speed.

Wind correction

To track the localiser in a crosswind, pilots point slightly into the wind. The wind correction angle is

$$\text{WCA} = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{V_w \sin\theta}{V_{\text{TAS}}}\right)$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between wind and course. Aircraft in the simulator apply this automatically once established.

Worked example 1: turn radius

A Rate One turn is 3°/s, so a full circle takes 120 s. At a true airspeed of 210 kt:

$$r = \frac{V}{\omega} = \frac{210\ \text{NM/h}}{(3^\circ/\text{s})(3600\ \text{s/h})\,\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}} = \frac{210}{188.5} \approx 1.11\ \text{NM}$$

A 90° turn onto the localiser therefore needs about 1.1 NM of lead, which is why intercepts at 30° are smoother: the aircraft only needs to turn 30°.

Worked example 2: true airspeed at altitude

Rule of thumb: TAS rises about 2% per 1,000 ft above sea level. At 8,000 ft with 210 kt indicated:

$$\text{TAS} \approx 210\,(1 + 0.02 \times 8) = 243.6\ \text{kt}$$

With a 12 kt headwind on final, groundspeed is about 232 kt. The simulator's data block shows groundspeed in tens of knots ("23").

Worked example 3: wake spacing and runway capacity

A Medium follows a Heavy on final: minimum 5 NM. At a final approach groundspeed of 140 kt:

$$t = \frac{5\ \text{NM}}{140\ \text{kt}} \times 3600 = 129\ \text{s}$$

Two Mediums at 3 NM need only 77 s. If every arrival were Medium-behind-Heavy, the runway could take at most $3600/129 \approx 28$ landings an hour instead of about 46. Sequencing similar types together increases capacity.

Worked example 4: descent planning

An arrival at FL150 (≈15,000 ft) must be at 8,000 ft by the 14 NM intercept fix. At 2,000 ft/min and 250 kt groundspeed:

$$t = \frac{15000 - 8000}{2000} = 3.5\ \text{min}, \qquad d = 250 \times \frac{3.5}{60} \approx 14.6\ \text{NM}$$

So start the descent at least about 29 NM from the airport (14.6 NM before the 14 NM fix), earlier if you also want to slow down: aircraft cannot easily descend and decelerate at once.
