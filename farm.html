Inputs & climate-smart options

1. Reference evapotranspiration, ET₀

Evapotranspiration (ET) is water lost from soil (evaporation) and through plants (transpiration). FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 (Allen et al., 1998) defines a reference rate ET₀ for a well-watered grass surface using the FAO Penman–Monteith equation. You will not need to evaluate it by hand; the idea is simply that hot, sunny, dry and windy days pull more water out of a field:

$$ET_0=\frac{0.408\,\Delta\,(R_n-G)+\gamma\,\frac{900}{T+273}\,u_2\,(e_s-e_a)}{\Delta+\gamma\,(1+0.34\,u_2)}\quad[\text{mm day}^{-1}]$$

It combines energy (net radiation $R_n$, soil heat flux $G$) with the drying power of the air (wind speed $u_2$ and vapour pressure deficit $e_s-e_a$); $\Delta$ is the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve and $\gamma$ the psychrometric constant. Units: $R_n$ and $G$ in MJ m⁻² day⁻¹, $T$ the mean air temperature at 2 m in °C, $u_2$ the wind speed at 2 m in m s⁻¹, vapour pressures in kPa, and $\Delta$, $\gamma$ in kPa °C⁻¹; the factor 0.408 converts MJ m⁻² to mm of evaporated water. In Kenya ET₀ is typically 3–6 mm per day. The lab uses typical monthly ET₀ values for each location, reduced on rainy, cloudy days.

2. Crop evapotranspiration and the crop coefficient

$$ET_c=K_c\times ET_0$$

The crop coefficient $K_c$ changes with growth stage: low in the initial stage (mostly soil evaporation), rising through development, peaking in mid-season when the canopy is full, then falling in the late season as the crop matures and dries. Mulch reduces the soil-evaporation part, so it lowers $K_c$ most while the canopy is still open.

3. The root-zone water balance

FAO-56 tracks the root-zone depletion $D_r$: how far the soil water is below field capacity, in mm. Each day

$$D_{r,i}=D_{r,i-1}-(P_i-RO_i)-I_{\text{net},i}+ET_{c,\text{adj},i}+DP_i$$

where $P$ is rain, $RO$ runoff, $I_{\text{net}}$ the irrigation water that actually reaches the root zone, and $DP$ deep percolation: water that drains below the roots when the soil is already at field capacity ($D_r$ cannot go below 0).

4. How much water can the soil store?

$$TAW=1000\,(\theta_{FC}-\theta_{WP})\,Z_r,\qquad RAW=p\cdot TAW$$

Field capacity $\theta_{FC}$ is the water content after free drainage; at the wilting point $\theta_{WP}$ roots can no longer extract water. TAW is the total available water in a root zone of depth $Z_r$ (m). Only the readily available water RAW, a fraction $p$ (≈0.4–0.6) of TAW, can be used without stress. FAO-56 adjusts $p$ for the evaporative demand: $p=p_{\text{table}}+0.04\,(5-ET_c)$.

5. Water stress

In words: while the soil still holds readily available water the crop is unstressed ($K_s=1$). Once depletion passes RAW, $K_s$ falls in a straight line to 0 at the wilting point, and the crop can use only $K_s$ times the water it needs.

$$K_s=\begin{cases}1 & D_r\le RAW\\[2pt] \dfrac{TAW-D_r}{(1-p)\,TAW} & D_r>RAW\end{cases},\qquad ET_{c,\text{adj}}=K_s\,K_c\,ET_0$$

6. From water to yield: FAO-33

Doorenbos and Kassam (FAO Paper 33, 1979) related relative yield loss to relative ET deficit through the yield response factor $K_y$:

$$1-\frac{Y_a}{Y_m}=K_y\left(1-\frac{ET_a}{ET_m}\right)$$

$K_y>1$ (maize ≈1.25) means yield falls proportionally faster than water use, so the crop is drought sensitive; $K_y<1$ (sorghum ≈0.9) means it is relatively tolerant. Stress at flowering is usually most damaging; the lab uses the seasonal $K_y$ for simplicity.

7. Runoff: the SCS curve number

$$RO=\frac{(P-0.2S)^2}{P+0.8S}\ \ (P>0.2S),\qquad S=\frac{25400}{CN}-254\ \text{mm}$$

A higher curve number (clay, crusted or bare soil) means more runoff. Mulch and conservation tillage improve infiltration, lowering CN.

8. Irrigation efficiency

$$I_{\text{gross}}=\frac{I_{\text{net}}}{E_a}$$

Typical application efficiencies: furrow ≈ 60%, sprinkler ≈ 75%, drip ≈ 90%. The losses are evaporation, wind drift, runoff and deep percolation. The lab's Refill at RAW schedule irrigates when $D_r\ge RAW$ and refills to field capacity.

9. Nitrogen: diminishing returns

Yield response to nitrogen is modelled with a Mitscherlich curve: each extra kilogram adds less than the last.

$$f(N)=1-(1-f_0)\,e^{-cN},\qquad N^*_{\text{econ}}=\frac1c\ln\!\left(\frac{Y_m\,f_w\,p_{\text{crop}}\,(1-f_0)\,c}{p_N}\right)$$

$f_0$ is the relative yield without fertiliser (low on depleted soils, high for legumes that fix nitrogen). The economic optimum $N^*$ is where the value of the extra yield equals the price of nitrogen $p_N$; with water stress ($f_w<1$) the optimum is lower.

Weather is generated randomly from typical monthly rainfall and ET₀ for each location. Prices and costs are round illustrative figures in KES and vary widely in practice.

Example 1: Crop water demand at mid-season

  1. Maize in mid-season: $K_c=1.20$. On a sunny April day in Machakos, $ET_0=5.0$ mm/day.
  2. $ET_c=1.20\times5.0=6.0$ mm/day, i.e. $6.0\times10=60$ m³ per hectare per day (1 mm over 1 ha = 10 m³).
  3. On a one-acre plot (0.405 ha): $60\times0.405=24$ m³ per day, about 1,200 twenty-litre jerricans.

Example 2: Available water and irrigation interval

  1. Loam: $\theta_{FC}=0.27$, $\theta_{WP}=0.12$, so $1000\times0.15=150$ mm per metre of soil.
  2. Full maize rooting depth $Z_r=1.0$ m: $TAW=150$ mm.
  3. $p=0.55+0.04\,(5-6.0)=0.51$, so $RAW=0.51\times150=76.5$ mm.
  4. With no rain, the crop uses RAW in $76.5/6.0\approx13$ days: the irrigation interval.

Example 3: Net and gross irrigation

  1. Refill the RAW: net irrigation $I_{\text{net}}=76.5$ mm.
  2. Drip ($E_a=0.90$): gross $=76.5/0.90=85$ mm. Furrow ($E_a=0.60$): $76.5/0.60=128$ mm.
  3. For one acre, drip needs $85\times10\times0.405=344$ m³; furrow needs 517 m³. At KES 15 per m³ pumped, that is KES 5,200 versus KES 7,800 per irrigation.

Example 4: Yield loss from water stress

  1. A rain-fed maize season ends with $ET_a=300$ mm and $ET_m=400$ mm: $ET_a/ET_m=0.75$.
  2. $1-Y_a/Y_m=1.25\times(1-0.75)=0.3125$, so $Y_a/Y_m=0.69$.
  3. With $Y_m=6.0$ t/ha and 50 kg N/ha ($f=1-0.6e^{-1.25}=0.83$): $Y_a=6.0\times0.69\times0.83=3.4$ t/ha.
  4. Sorghum ($K_y=0.9$) with the same ET ratio would keep $1-0.9\times0.25=78\%$ of its potential.

Example 5: Economic optimum nitrogen for maize

  1. $Y_m=6000$ kg/ha, price KES 45/kg, $f_0=0.40$, $c=0.025$, N at KES 150/kg, no water stress ($f_w=1$).
  2. $N^*=\frac{1}{0.025}\ln\frac{6000\times45\times0.6\times0.025}{150}=40\ln 27=132$ kg N/ha.
  3. In a dry season with $f_w=0.6$: $N^*=40\ln(16.2)=111$ kg N/ha. Less water, less fertiliser pays.

Climate-smart agriculture (CSA)

CSA aims at three things at once: higher productivity, better resilience to climate shocks, and lower emissions where possible. The options in the lab are widely promoted in Kenya:

Mulching

A layer of crop residue shades the soil, cutting evaporation while the canopy is open and reducing runoff and erosion. In the model it lowers the soil-evaporation part of $K_c$ and the curve number.

Conservation tillage

Minimum tillage, ripping and planting basins (including zai pits) keep soil structure and organic matter, improving infiltration and water storage. The model raises TAW slightly and lowers runoff, and swaps ploughing costs for herbicide and labour.

Drought-tolerant varieties

Early-maturing, drought-tolerant varieties escape end-of-season drought and lose less yield per unit of water deficit (lower $K_y$), at the cost of a slightly lower yield potential.

Water-harvesting pans

Lined farm ponds collect runoff from fields, roads and roofs during storms for supplementary irrigation in dry spells. A 150 m³ pan stores only about 37 mm over one acre, so it is best used for high-value crops or critical growth stages.

Integrated pest management

Regular scouting, early action, push–pull systems and targeted sprays reduce losses from pests such as fall armyworm, which reached Kenya in 2017, while limiting pesticide use.

Timely planting

Planting at the onset of the rains lets the crop use the whole season. Late planting often pushes flowering into the dry spell at the end of the rains.
