Three ears in one

The outer ear (pinna and external auditory canal) collects sound and funnels it to the tympanic membrane. The air-filled middle ear houses the three ossicles, which transfer vibration to the fluid-filled inner ear. The inner ear contains two sensory organs that share the same fluids and nerve: the cochlea for hearing and the vestibular system (semicircular canals, utricle and saccule) for balance.

Why the middle ear exists: impedance matching

Sound in air must enter the cochlear fluids, which have a far higher characteristic acoustic impedance ($Z = \rho c$). At a plane air–water boundary the fraction of incident intensity transmitted is

$$T = \frac{4 Z_1 Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2}, \qquad Z_\text{air} \approx 413\ \text{Pa}\,\text{s}\,\text{m}^{-1},\quad Z_\text{water} \approx 1.48\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}\,\text{s}\,\text{m}^{-1}$$

which gives $T \approx 1.1\times10^{-3}$: only about 0.1 % of the energy, a loss of roughly 30 dB. The middle ear recovers most of this with two mechanical tricks:

  • Area ratio. Force collected over the effective eardrum area (about 55 mm²) is delivered to the much smaller stapes footplate (about 3.2 mm²): $A_\text{TM}/A_\text{FP} \approx 17$.
  • Ossicular lever. The malleus handle is about 1.3 times longer than the long process of the incus, so force increases by $\approx 1.3$ while displacement decreases.
$$\frac{p_\text{oval window}}{p_\text{eardrum}} \approx \frac{A_\text{TM}}{A_\text{FP}} \times \frac{l_\text{malleus}}{l_\text{incus}} \approx 17 \times 1.3 \approx 22 \;\Rightarrow\; 20\log_{10}22 \approx 27\ \text{dB}$$
This is the classical textbook estimate. Measured middle-ear gain depends on frequency (it is largest around 1 kHz and smaller at high frequencies) because the eardrum does not move as a rigid piston. The sound-journey lab uses the fixed 22× figure for clarity.

Decibels

Hearing spans about 12 orders of magnitude in intensity, so we use a logarithmic scale. Sound pressure level and intensity level are

$$L_p = 20\log_{10}\frac{p_\text{rms}}{p_0},\ \ p_0 = 20\ \mu\text{Pa} \qquad L_I = 10\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0},\ \ I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2,\qquad I = \frac{p_\text{rms}^2}{\rho c}$$

+6 dB doubles the pressure, +10 dB multiplies intensity by 10 (and is heard as roughly twice as loud), and +20 dB multiplies pressure by 10. Quiet library ≈ 40 dB, conversation ≈ 60 dB, busy matatu stage ≈ 85 dB, nightclub ≈ 100 dB, pain threshold ≈ 120–130 dB.

Hearing range and the threshold curve

Young healthy listeners hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Sensitivity is greatest at 2–5 kHz, partly because the 2.5 cm ear canal is a tube closed at one end (the eardrum) and resonates at a quarter wavelength:

$$f_\text{res} = \frac{c}{4L} = \frac{343}{4\times0.025} \approx 3.4\ \text{kHz}$$

The lab uses Terhardt's approximation of the threshold in quiet ($f$ in kHz): $T(f) = 3.64 f^{-0.8} - 6.5\,e^{-0.6(f-3.3)^2} + 10^{-3}f^4$ dB SPL. The upper frequency limit falls with age (presbycusis), so many adults cannot hear above 14–16 kHz.

The cochlea: a mechanical frequency analyser

The cochlea is a 35 mm fluid-filled tube coiled about 2.5 turns and divided lengthwise by the cochlear duct (scala media). The stapes pushes on the oval window into the scala vestibuli; the round window bulges out of the scala tympani to relieve the pressure. The pressure difference across the cochlear partition launches a travelling wave along the basilar membrane. Near the base the membrane is narrow (≈ 0.1 mm) and stiff; at the apex it is wide (≈ 0.5 mm) and floppy. Each place therefore resonates best at one characteristic frequency: high frequencies peak near the base, low frequencies near the apex (tonotopy). Greenwood's function maps position to frequency in humans:

$$f = 165.4\left(10^{2.1x} - 0.88\right)\ \text{Hz}, \qquad x = \frac{\text{distance from apex}}{\text{cochlear length}}\in[0,1]$$

Inverting it gives $x = \tfrac{1}{2.1}\log_{10}\!\left(\tfrac{f}{165.4} + 0.88\right)$, which is what places the peak in Lab 2.

Hair cells and transduction

On the basilar membrane sits the organ of Corti: one row of about 3,500 inner hair cells (the true sensory cells, contacted by about 95 % of afferent fibres) and three rows of about 12,000 outer hair cells (the "cochlear amplifier", which change length electrically via the motor protein prestin and sharpen tuning). Shearing between the basilar and tectorial membranes deflects the stereocilia. Deflection towards the tallest row stretches tip links, opening mechano-electrical transduction channels; K⁺ flows in from the K⁺-rich endolymph (driven by the +80 mV endocochlear potential generated by the stria vascularis), depolarising the cell and releasing glutamate onto auditory nerve fibres. Below about 4 kHz fibres fire in step with the waveform (phase locking); level is coded by firing rate and by recruitment of more fibres.

Balance: semicircular canals and otolith organs

Three semicircular canals at roughly right angles detect angular acceleration about any axis. When the head starts to rotate, the endolymph lags behind because of inertia, bending the gelatinous cupula in the ampulla and its hair cells. Hair cells fire about 90 spikes/s at rest, so they signal both directions (push–pull pairs between left and right ears). During constant rotation, the elastic cupula returns to rest within about 6 s (time constant τ) and the sensation of rotation fades; when rotation stops the fluid overshoots and you feel turning in the opposite direction (post-rotatory vertigo and nystagmus). The utricle and saccule carry heavy calcium carbonate crystals (otoconia) and sense linear acceleration and head tilt relative to gravity.

Reading an audiogram

Pure-tone audiometry measures the quietest tone a person can hear at each frequency, in dB HL (hearing level: 0 dB HL is the average threshold of young normal listeners at that frequency, so the curved threshold of Lab 2 is flattened). Lower on the chart means worse hearing. Air conduction (headphones) tests the whole pathway; bone conduction (a vibrator on the mastoid) bypasses the outer and middle ear.

Tuning-fork tests (512 Hz)

Rinne: normally air conduction is louder than bone (positive). In conductive loss, bone is louder in the affected ear (negative). Weber: fork on the forehead lateralises to the ear with conductive loss, or away from the ear with sensorineural loss.

1. Where does a 1 kHz tone peak?

  1. Greenwood inverted: $x = \tfrac{1}{2.1}\log_{10}\!\left(\tfrac{1000}{165.4} + 0.88\right) = \tfrac{1}{2.1}\log_{10}(6.926)$.
  2. $\log_{10} 6.926 = 0.8405$, so $x = 0.400$ of the length measured from the apex.
  3. Distance from the base (oval window) $= (1-0.400)\times35\ \text{mm} = 21.0$ mm. Set Lab 2 to 1 kHz and the HUD reads 21.0 mm.

2. Which frequency peaks 10 mm from the base?

  1. From the apex: $35-10 = 25$ mm, so $x = 25/35 = 0.714$.
  2. $f = 165.4\,(10^{2.1\times0.714} - 0.88) = 165.4\,(31.62 - 0.88) \approx 5.08$ kHz.
  3. Noise-induced damage (typically 3–6 kHz) therefore lies in the first third of the basal turn.

3. Middle-ear pressure gain

  1. Area ratio $55/3.2 = 17.2$; lever ratio $1.3$.
  2. Pressure gain $17.2\times1.3 = 22.3$, i.e. $20\log_{10}22.3 = 27$ dB.
  3. A 60 dB SPL tone has $p = 20\,\mu\text{Pa}\times10^{60/20} = 0.020$ Pa at the eardrum, so about $0.020\times22.3 = 0.45$ Pa at the oval window (Lab 2 shows the same).

4. Adding sound sources

  1. Two uncorrelated sources of 80 dB each: intensities add, $I = 2\times10^{8}\,I_0$.
  2. $L = 10\log_{10}(2\times10^{8}) = 80 + 3.0 = 83$ dB, not 160 dB.
  3. Halving your distance from a point source quadruples intensity: +6 dB. Exposure limits often use a 3 dB exchange rate: 85 dB for 8 h is about as risky as 88 dB for 4 h or 100 dB for 15 min.

5. Ear canal resonance

  1. A tube open at one end and closed at the other resonates when $L = \lambda/4$.
  2. $f = 343/(4\times0.025) = 3430$ Hz, close to the dip in the threshold curve in Lab 2 (lowest threshold near 3.3–3.5 kHz).
