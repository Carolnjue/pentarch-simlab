1. Waves and superposition

A travelling wave carries a disturbance at speed $v = f\lambda$. A circular wave from a point source in a ripple tank is $\psi(r,t) = \frac{A}{\sqrt r}\cos(kr - \omega t + \phi)$, with wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ (the $1/\sqrt r$ spreads the energy over a growing circle). Where waves overlap, their displacements add: the principle of superposition.

2. Two-source interference

Two coherent sources a distance $d$ apart produce, at any point, a path difference $\Delta r = r_2 - r_1$. In phase sources interfere

$$\text{constructively when } \Delta r = m\lambda, \qquad \text{destructively when } \Delta r = \left(m+\tfrac12\right)\lambda$$

The nodal (destructive) lines are hyperbolae with the sources as foci; far away they approach straight lines at angles $d\sin\theta = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda$. The number of nodal lines on each side is set by $d/\lambda$. A phase difference $\phi$ between the sources shifts the whole pattern, which is the principle behind phased-array antennas and radar.

3. Young's double slit, single slit and gratings

For light the slits act as coherent sources. With slit width $a$, separation $d$ and $N$ slits, the far-field (Fraunhofer) intensity is the product of a single-slit envelope and a multi-slit interference term:

$$\begin{gathered}I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\left(\frac{\sin N\alpha}{N\sin\alpha}\right)^2\\ \beta = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\end{gathered}$$
  • Maxima (principal): $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$. For small angles on a screen at distance $L$, the fringe spacing is $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$.
  • Single-slit minima: $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$ ($m \ne 0$). The central maximum has width $2\lambda L/a$, so narrower slits spread light more.
  • Missing orders occur when an interference maximum falls on an envelope zero, i.e. when $d/a$ is an integer ratio.
  • Gratings (large $N$) make the principal maxima very sharp, with $N-2$ weak subsidiary maxima between them. That is why gratings are used in spectrometers: the resolving power is $\lambda/\Delta\lambda = mN$.

The lab plots exactly this formula, so its peaks and zeros match the theory. Switch on photon build-up to see that the pattern is a probability distribution: single photons land at random, but with probability proportional to $I$.

4. Lenses and mirrors

Sign convention used in this lab: "real is positive". Distances are measured from the lens or mirror. The object distance $u$ is positive for a real object; the image distance $v$ is positive for a real image (on the far side of a lens, or in front of a mirror) and negative for a virtual image. The focal length $f$ is positive for converging elements (convex lens, concave mirror) and negative for diverging ones (concave lens, convex mirror).
$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}, \qquad \text{magnification } m = \frac{v}{u} = \frac{h_i}{h_o}\ \text{(size ratio)}$$

With this convention a real image is always inverted and a virtual image upright. (Many university texts instead use the Cartesian convention, measuring all distances in the direction light travels, which gives $\frac1f = \frac1v - \frac1u$ with $u$ negative for a real object. The physics is identical; only the bookkeeping differs.) For a spherical mirror $f = R/2$.

Principal rays for a thin lens: (1) a ray parallel to the axis leaves through the far focal point $F'$ (or appears to come from the near one for a diverging lens); (2) a ray through the optical centre is undeviated; (3) a ray through the near focal point $F$ leaves parallel to the axis. Where the rays (or their backward extensions) meet is the image.

Two-lens instruments. The image from the first lens is the object for the second. A compound microscope uses a short-focus objective to form a magnified real image just inside the focal length of the eyepiece, which acts as a magnifier: overall $m = m_1 m_2$. A refracting telescope places the lenses $f_o + f_e$ apart so parallel light leaves parallel (afocal), with angular magnification $M = -f_o/f_e$.

5. Refraction, Snell's law and total internal reflection

$$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2, \qquad n = \frac{c}{v}, \qquad \sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}\ (n_1 > n_2)$$

Light bends towards the normal when it slows down. Going from a denser to a less dense medium, beyond the critical angle $\theta_c$ no refracted ray exists and all the light is reflected: total internal reflection. The partial reflection below $\theta_c$ follows the Fresnel equations (the lab shows the unpolarised average); it grows sharply near $\theta_c$.

Optical fibres trap light by total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary. Rays entering the end face within the acceptance cone are guided:

$$\text{NA} = \sin\theta_{max} = \sqrt{n_{core}^2 - n_{clad}^2}$$

Fibres carry most of the world's internet traffic, including the submarine cables that land on the Kenyan coast at Mombasa.

6. Standing waves and harmonics

Two equal waves travelling in opposite directions add to a standing wave $y = 2A\sin kx\,\cos\omega t$ with fixed nodes (no motion) and antinodes (maximum motion) half a wavelength apart. Fitting whole patterns into a length $L$ gives the allowed frequencies:

$$\begin{gathered}\text{string fixed at both ends: } f_n = \frac{n v}{2L},\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}},\quad n = 1,2,3,\dots\\ \text{pipe open at both ends: } f_n = \frac{n v}{2L}, \qquad \text{pipe closed at one end: } f_n = \frac{n v}{4L},\ n = 1,3,5,\dots\end{gathered}$$

The speed of sound in air is about $v \approx 331.3\sqrt{1 + T_C/273.15}$ m/s (343 m/s at 20 °C). A plucked or blown instrument sounds many harmonics at once; their relative strengths give its timbre. Plucking a string at a fraction $p$ of its length gives harmonic amplitudes proportional to $\sin(n\pi p)/n^2$, so harmonics with a node at the plucking point are missing. Open ends are displacement antinodes (pressure nodes); a closed end is a displacement node. Real pipes need a small end correction (about $0.6r$ per open end), which the lab neglects.

Example 1: Young's fringes

Red laser light ($\lambda = 600$ nm) passes through two slits $d = 0.25$ mm apart onto a screen $L = 1.5$ m away.

  1. Fringe spacing $\Delta y = \lambda L/d = (600\times10^{-9})(1.5)/(0.25\times10^{-3}) = 3.6\times10^{-3}$ m $= 3.6$ mm.
  2. Angle of the first maximum: $\sin\theta_1 = \lambda/d = 0.0024$, so $\theta_1 = 0.138^\circ$: small angles really are small.
  3. If each slit is $a = 0.05$ mm wide, the envelope's first zero is at $\lambda L/a = 18$ mm, so about $2 \times 18/3.6 - 1 = 9$ bright fringes fit in the central envelope (the 5th-order fringes coincide with the zeros: missing orders, since $d/a = 5$).

Example 2: a diffraction grating

A grating with 600 lines/mm is lit by a green 532 nm laser.

  1. $d = 1/600$ mm $= 1.667$ µm.
  2. First order: $\sin\theta_1 = \lambda/d = 0.3192$, $\theta_1 = 18.6^\circ$.
  3. Highest order: $m_{max} = \lfloor d/\lambda \rfloor = \lfloor 3.13 \rfloor = 3$, at $\theta_3 = \sin^{-1}(0.958) = 73.3^\circ$.

Example 3: a projector lens

An object is 15 cm from a converging lens with $f = 10$ cm. Using real-is-positive:

  1. $\frac{1}{v} = \frac1f - \frac1u = \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{15} = \frac{1}{30}$, so $v = +30$ cm: a real image 30 cm beyond the lens.
  2. $m = v/u = 30/15 = 2$: twice the size, and (being real) inverted.
  3. Move the object to 7 cm (inside $f$): $\frac1v = \frac1{10} - \frac17$, $v = -23.3$ cm. Negative means virtual, on the same side as the object, upright and magnified $3.3\times$: a magnifying glass.

Example 4: critical angles and a fibre

  1. Glass to air: $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(1/1.50) = 41.8^\circ$. Water to air: $48.8^\circ$. Diamond: only $24.4^\circ$, which is why diamonds sparkle: light is trapped and bounces around inside.
  2. Air to water at $40^\circ$: $\sin\theta_2 = \sin40^\circ/1.33$, $\theta_2 = 28.9^\circ$.
  3. A fibre with $n_{core} = 1.48$ and $n_{clad} = 1.46$ has $\text{NA} = \sqrt{1.48^2 - 1.46^2} = 0.242$, an acceptance half-angle of $14.0^\circ$ in air.

Example 5: tuning a guitar string

A high-E string has $L = 0.650$ m and $\mu = 0.40$ g/m. What tension gives E4 = 329.6 Hz?

  1. Required wave speed $v = 2Lf_1 = 2(0.650)(329.6) = 428.5$ m/s.
  2. $T = \mu v^2 = (0.40\times10^{-3})(428.5)^2 = 73.4$ N (the weight of about 7.5 kg).
  3. Second harmonic: $659$ Hz; fretting at half length doubles every frequency (one octave up).

Example 6: open and closed pipes

  1. At 20 °C, $v = 331.3\sqrt{1 + 20/273.15} = 343.2$ m/s.
  2. A 0.50 m pipe open at both ends: $f_1 = v/2L = 343$ Hz, with harmonics 686, 1030 Hz, …
  3. The same pipe closed at one end: $f_1 = v/4L = 172$ Hz, an octave lower, and only odd harmonics (515, 858 Hz, …), which gives the hollow sound of a clarinet-like tone.

Open experiments

  1. Counting nodal lines. In the ripple tank, double $d$ at fixed $\lambda$. How does the number of nodal lines change? Predict it from $d/\lambda$ first.
  2. Colour and spacing. With a double slit, compare violet (400 nm) and red (700 nm). Which spreads more? This is why a grating splits white light into a spectrum with red furthest out.
  3. Real or virtual? Slide the object through $2f$ and $f$ in front of a converging lens and record $v$ and $m$. Plot $1/v$ against $1/u$: a straight line of gradient −1 and intercept $1/f$.
  4. Plucking position. Pluck the string at 0.5 of its length and listen. Which harmonics are missing in the spectrum, and why?
