About the model. The simulator integrates the longitudinal (pitch-plane) equations of motion 240 times a second: two translational equations, the pitching-moment equation and a ground-contact model. Aerodynamic coefficients come from your design using lifting-line theory, component drag build-up and classical stability estimates. It is a 2-D model: there is no roll or yaw, no wind and no propeller slipstream, and the post-stall behaviour is approximate.

1. The four forces

In the pitch plane the aircraft has velocity $V$ along a flight path inclined at $\gamma$, with pitch attitude $\theta$ and angle of attack $\alpha = \theta - \gamma$. Lift acts perpendicular to the velocity, drag opposite to it, thrust along the body and weight straight down:

$$m\dot V = T\cos\alpha - D - W\sin\gamma, \qquad mV\dot\gamma = L + T\sin\alpha - W\cos\gamma$$
$$I_{yy}\,\dot q = M = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\bar c\, C_m, \qquad \dot\theta = q$$

2. The lift equation and stall speed

$$L = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S C_L \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_s = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S C_{L,max}}}$$

In level flight $L = W$, so flying slower needs a higher $C_L$ and therefore a higher angle of attack. At $C_{L,max}$ the wing stalls; that sets the minimum flying speed. Flaps raise $C_{L,max}$ and lower the stall speed for take-off and landing. Because $\rho$ appears in the denominator, the stall speed (as true airspeed) is higher at a hot, high airfield such as Nairobi than at the coast. The wing loading $W/S$ is the single most important number for slow-speed performance.

The wing's lift curve slope follows from lifting-line theory, $a = a_0/(1 + a_0/(\pi e AR))$ with $a_0 = 0.9\times2\pi$ and $e = 0.8$, and the wing's lift curve rounds off near the stall so that its peak clean $C_{L,max}$ is about 90% of the section value.

3. Drag and power required

$$C_D = C_{D0} + kC_L^2, \qquad k = \frac{1}{\pi e AR}$$

$C_{D0}$ is built up from components: the wing (section drag), the fuselage (turbulent skin friction $C_f = 0.455/(\log_{10}Re)^{2.58}$ times a form factor and wetted area), the tail and the landing gear. The power needed for level flight is

$$P_{req} = DV = \underbrace{\tfrac12\rho V^3 S C_{D0}}_{\text{parasite}} + \underbrace{\frac{2kW^2}{\rho V S}}_{\text{induced}}$$

Parasite power grows as $V^3$; induced power falls as $1/V$. The sum has a minimum: the speed for minimum power (best endurance and minimum sink in a glide). Minimum drag (best glide angle and, for propeller aircraft, best range) occurs at a higher speed, where the tangent from the origin touches the $P_{req}$ curve:

$$\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{max} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{kC_{D0}}}, \qquad V_{md} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S}}\left(\frac{k}{C_{D0}}\right)^{1/4}$$

The excess power sets the climb rate, $V_y = (P_{avail}-P_{req})/W$, and the right-hand crossing of the two curves is the maximum level speed. A propeller's thrust is modelled as $T = \eta_p P/\sqrt{V^2 + V_c^2}$, which matches the static thrust from actuator-disc theory at $V=0$ and $\eta_p P/V$ at speed. Jet thrust is nearly constant with speed. Both fall with air density.

4. Take-off and range

$$s_g \approx \frac{V_{LO}^2}{2\bar a}, \qquad \bar a = \frac{1}{m}\Big[T - D - \mu(W - L)\Big]_{0.7V_{LO}}, \qquad V_{LO} = 1.2\,V_{s}$$

Breguet's range equation for a propeller aircraft, with propeller efficiency $\eta_p$ and brake specific fuel consumption $c$ (expressed per unit weight of fuel), is

$$R = \frac{\eta_p}{c}\,\frac{L}{D}\,\ln\frac{W_0}{W_1}$$

5. Longitudinal static stability

An aircraft is statically stable in pitch if a gust that raises the angle of attack produces a nose-down moment: $C_{m\alpha} < 0$. The tail makes this happen. The point about which $C_m$ does not change with $\alpha$ is the neutral point:

$$\frac{x_{np}}{\bar c} = \frac{x_{ac}}{\bar c} + \eta\,V_H\,\frac{a_t}{a}\left(1 - \frac{d\varepsilon}{d\alpha}\right) - \frac{\Delta C_{m\alpha,fus}}{a}, \qquad V_H = \frac{S_h l_t}{S\bar c}$$

with downwash gradient $d\varepsilon/d\alpha \approx 2a/(\pi AR)$. The static margin is the distance from the centre of gravity to the neutral point in chords, and $C_{m\alpha} = -a\cdot SM$:

$$SM = \frac{x_{np} - x_{cg}}{\bar c}$$

Typical light aircraft fly with 5–20%. A negative margin (CG behind the neutral point) is unstable: any disturbance grows and the pilot must fight it continuously. A very large margin is stable but sluggish; the elevator may not be able to raise the nose for take-off or landing. Try both in the simulator. Move the payload forward and backward and watch the CG marker cross the neutral point.

6. Pitch control and damping

The elevator adds $C_{m\delta}\,\delta_e$ with $C_{m\delta} \approx -\tau\,\eta\,a_t V_H$. In trim, $C_m = 0$, so each elevator setting corresponds to one angle of attack and hence (in level flight) one airspeed: the elevator is really a speed control. The tail also damps pitching motion: $C_{mq} \approx -2\eta a_t V_H\,l_t/\bar c$. Together these give the short-period oscillation you feel when you jab the elevator.

Investigations

  1. Hot and high. Take the Trainer, note the take-off roll at Mombasa ISA, then at Nairobi ISA+20. Explain the difference using the density ratio and $V_s \propto 1/\sqrt\rho$.
  2. Glide ratio. In the Glide mission, hold the best-glide speed shown in the hangar and estimate your glide ratio from height lost and distance covered. Fly 15 knots faster and slower and compare.
  3. Find the back of the power curve. In level flight, slow down to just above the stall. Why do you need more throttle, not less, to stay level?
  4. Aspect ratio. Keep wing area fixed but double the span (halve the chord). How do best L/D, stall speed and climb rate change? Why do gliders look the way they do?
  5. Unstable aircraft. Move the payload aft until the static margin is negative, then fly. Describe what happens when you let go of the elevator.
