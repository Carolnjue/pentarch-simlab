The eye as an optical instrument

Light is refracted at four surfaces before reaching the retina: the front and back of the cornea and the front and back of the crystalline lens. The cornea provides about two-thirds of the eye's power (≈ 43 D), because the jump in refractive index from air (1.000) to cornea (1.376) is by far the largest. The lens (≈ 20 D relaxed) is the adjustable part. The whole relaxed Le Grand schematic eye used in Lab 2 has a power of 59.9 D and an axial length of 24.2 mm.

$$P = \frac{1}{f}\ \ (\text{dioptres, D} = \text{m}^{-1}), \qquad P_\text{surface} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}, \qquad P_\text{cornea front} = \frac{1.3771 - 1}{0.0078\ \text{m}} = 48.3\ \text{D}$$

We describe light by its vergence $L = n/l$, where $l$ is the signed distance to the point the light comes from or goes to (negative for real objects in front of the lens). A thin lens adds its power: $L' = L + P$. Light from an object 25 cm away has $L = -4$ D; from 6 m or more, $L \approx 0$.

Accommodation

To focus near objects the ciliary muscle contracts, the zonules slacken and the elastic lens becomes rounder (mainly the front surface), increasing its power. The amplitude of accommodation falls with age as the lens stiffens; Hofstetter's average formula is $A \approx 18.5 - 0.3\times\text{age}$ D. The far point is the farthest point seen clearly with relaxed accommodation; the near point is the closest with maximum accommodation:

$$L_\text{far} = R_x, \qquad L_\text{near} = L_\text{far} - A, \qquad \text{near point distance} = \frac{1}{|L_\text{near}|}$$

Here $R_x$ is the refractive error expressed as the correcting power (negative for myopes, positive for hyperopes), so a −2.50 D myope has its far point where light has vergence −2.50 D, i.e. 40 cm in front of the eye.

A correcting lens must form an image of a distant object at the eye's far point. For a spectacle lens at vertex distance $d$ from the cornea and a far point at distance $l_\text{far}$ from the cornea:

$$F_\text{spectacle} = \frac{1}{l_\text{far} + d}\qquad(\text{signed distances in metres; } l_\text{far} < 0 \text{ for myopes},\ d > 0)$$

Each 1 mm of extra axial length makes an eye about 2.7 D more myopic. Most myopia is axial; its rapid rise worldwide is linked to more near work and less time outdoors in childhood.

Astigmatism

In an astigmatic eye one meridian (say vertical) is more curved and focuses in front of the other. A point becomes two perpendicular focal lines separated by the "interval of Sturm", with a circle of least confusion between them. A cylindrical lens has power only perpendicular to its axis, so a −2.00 D cylinder at axis 180° weakens just the vertical meridian. Prescriptions are written sphere / cylinder × axis, e.g. −1.50 / −2.00 × 180. In Lab 2 the upper rays show the vertical meridian and the lower rays the horizontal meridian.

The pupil and depth of field

The iris sphincter (parasympathetic) constricts the pupil in bright light; the dilator (sympathetic) widens it in the dark, from about 2 to 8 mm (Lab 2 uses the Moon–Spencer formula $D = 4.9 - 3\tanh(0.4\log_{10}L)$ mm, $L$ in cd/m²). The angular size of a blur circle is roughly pupil diameter × defocus: $\beta \approx D_\text{pupil}\,|\Delta L|$ radians. Small pupils give a larger depth of field, which is why people with uncorrected myopia squint and why reading is easier in bright light.

The retina

The retina is "inverted": light passes through the ganglion and bipolar cell layers before reaching the photoreceptors, which face the pigment epithelium. Two photoreceptor systems give us duplex vision:

  • Rods (≈ 92 million): very sensitive (can respond to a single photon), one pigment (rhodopsin, peak ≈ 498 nm), dim-light (scotopic) vision, no colour, absent from the central fovea, peak density about 20° out.
  • Cones (≈ 4.6 million): daylight (photopic) vision, high acuity and colour; three types with pigment peaks near 420 nm (S), 534 nm (M) and 564 nm (L). Packed at up to ~200,000/mm² in the fovea, where the inner layers are pushed aside to form a pit.

About 1.2 million ganglion cell axons converge at the optic disc, where there are no photoreceptors: the blind spot, about 15° nasal to the fovea (so it appears 15° to the temporal side in the visual field). The brain fills it in, so you never notice it.

Colour vision and colour blindness

Colour comes from comparing the outputs of the three cone types (trichromacy) and is then recoded as opponent channels: red–green (L − M), blue–yellow (S − (L + M)) and light–dark (L + M). The genes for the L and M pigments lie on the X chromosome, so red–green deficiencies are X-linked and far commoner in males (about 8 % of men of northern European ancestry and about 2–4 % in many African populations, against < 0.5 % of women). Protan defects affect L cones, deutan defects M cones and tritan defects (rare, not sex-linked) S cones.

1. Prescribing for a myope

  1. A student sees clearly only up to 40 cm: far point $l_\text{far} = -0.40$ m from the cornea.
  2. Spectacle lens 12 mm in front: $F = 1/(l_\text{far} - d) = 1/(-0.40 + 0.012) = 1/(-0.388) = -2.58$ D, prescribed as −2.50 D (nearest 0.25 D).
  3. A contact lens sits on the cornea ($d = 0$): $F = 1/(-0.40) = -2.50$ D. Minus lenses need slightly less power the closer they are to the eye.

2. Axial myopia in Lab 2

  1. Set the axial length to 25.6 mm (1.4 mm longer than the emmetropic 24.2 mm).
  2. The lab reports a prescription of about −3.75 D, i.e. ≈ −2.7 D per millimetre.
  3. Far point at the spectacle plane: $1/3.73 = 0.268$ m, i.e. about 28 cm from the cornea. Wear −3.75 D and the image returns to the retina.

3. Presbyopia and a reading addition

  1. Age 50: $A \approx 18.5 - 0.3\times50 = 3.5$ D. Near point $= 1/3.5 = 0.29$ m.
  2. Reading at 40 cm needs 2.5 D. Comfortably we use only about half the amplitude (1.75 D), so we need an addition of $2.5 - 1.75 = +0.75$ D.
  3. At 60, $A \approx 0.5$ D: an addition of about +2.25 D is needed to read at 40 cm, and about +3.75 D for 25 cm: the page is only 23.8 cm from a spectacle lens worn 12 mm in front of the eye, so the lens must supply $1/0.238 - 0.5 \approx 3.7$ D (try it in Lab 2: age 60, object 25 cm, auto-accommodation on).

4. How blurred is 1 dioptre?

  1. Pupil 4 mm, defocus 1 D: $\beta = 0.004 \times 1 = 0.004$ rad $= 0.004\times\frac{180}{\pi}\times60 = 13.8$ arcmin.
  2. Normal acuity (6/6) resolves 1 arcmin detail: 1 D of blur is very noticeable. With a 2 mm pupil it halves to 6.9 arcmin.

5. Focal length of the eye

  1. Equivalent power 59.9 D. In air $f = 1/59.9 = 16.7$ mm.
  2. Inside the eye (vitreous $n' = 1.336$): $f' = n'/P = 1.336/59.9 = 22.3$ mm behind the second principal point, which lies about 1.9 mm behind the cornea, putting the focus at 24.2 mm: on the retina.
