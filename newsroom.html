    What makes something news?

    Editors make dozens of fast judgements every hour about what deserves airtime. The classic framework (Galtung & Ruge, 1965; updated by Harcup & O'Neill, 2001 and 2017) lists news values: properties that make a story more likely to be selected and placed higher. The simulator uses six of the most widely taught:

    Proximity

    Geographic or cultural closeness to the audience. A burst water main in Eastlands matters more to a Nairobi station than a larger one abroad.

    Impact

    How many people are affected and how seriously. A fuel price rise touches every household through transport and food costs.

    Timeliness

    New, happening now, or about to happen. "From midnight tonight" beats "last month".

    Prominence

    Involves well-known people or institutions: a governor, an MP, the President, a national team.

    Conflict

    Disagreement, protest, court cases, competition. Conflict gives a story tension, but should not be manufactured.

    Human interest

    Stories that move us through real people's experiences: families in an evacuation centre, a rescued child.

    A simple editorial model scores a story by how many values it carries, with impact weighted most heavily because it measures public consequence. The simulator uses a weighted sum like this to rank stories:

    $$W = 1.5\,I + P + T + Pr + C + H \qquad (\text{each term } 0 \text{ or } 1)$$

    Real editors do not literally add numbers, but the model shows why a story that is local, consequential and new (a flood displacing 1,800 households today) leads a bulletin ahead of a celebrity event, and why a corporate product launch with no public consequence is usually spiked.

    News value never overrides accuracy. A sensational claim that is false has zero value, and broadcasting it carries legal and reputational cost. Verification comes before ranking.

    Running orders

    A bulletin running order puts the strongest story first (the lead), then descends roughly by importance, grouping related items (all flood stories together) and ending with lighter items or sport. The editor also manages time: a five-minute radio bulletin might carry a 90-second lead and four or five 30–60-second stories. Total duration must hit the slot: overrunning pushes adverts and the next programme.

    $$T_{\text{bulletin}} = \sum_i d_i \approx T_{\text{slot}} \pm 20\ \text{s}$$

    The inverted pyramid

    Hard news is written with the most important facts first and detail in descending order of importance, so the story still makes sense if it is cut from the bottom. The first sentence, the lede (or intro), should answer as many of the 5Ws and H as possible: Who, What, Where, When, Why and How.

    Headlines

    • Short: broadcast and web headlines usually stay under about 70 characters (roughly 6–12 words) so they fit on a screen or a ticker.
    • Active voice, present tense: "Floods displace 1,800 families" rather than "1,800 families were displaced by floods".
    • Accurate and supported by the story: a headline can be defamatory on its own even if the body is careful.
    • No sensational adjectives ("horror", "chaos", "shocking"). Let the facts carry the weight.

    Attribution

    Every claim the newsroom has not witnessed itself must be attributed: "…the County Commissioner said", "according to the Red Cross". Attribution tells the audience how you know and protects the outlet legally. Broadcast style often puts attribution first: "Police say…".

    Readability

    Copy desks often check readability. The Flesch Reading Ease score rises as sentences and words get shorter:

    $$\text{FRE} = 206.835 - 1.015\,\frac{\text{words}}{\text{sentences}} - 84.6\,\frac{\text{syllables}}{\text{words}}$$

    News copy aimed at a general audience typically scores 60–70. A 25-word lede with simple words lands comfortably there; a 50-word lede stuffed with jargon falls below 30. The simulator's copy checker flags ledes over 35 words.

    Verify before you amplify

    Information reaches a newsroom through four main channels, each with typical risks:

    ChannelTypical reliabilityMain risks
    Wire agencyHigh: professional standards, corrections policyErrors still happen; check figures and names
    Press releaseAccurate on facts, but self-interestedSpin, puffery, omission of bad news; could be fake letterhead
    Tip (phone, email)Varies widelyGrudges, rumours, defamation, hoaxes
    Social mediaLowest by defaultRecycled images, parody accounts, deliberate disinformation

    The verification toolkit used in the simulator

    1. Source check (provenance). Who posted or sent this? Account age, verification, history of accuracy, a real name and contact, whether they were in a position to know.
    2. Reverse image search. Upload the image or a video keyframe to reverse-search engines. If the earliest copy predates the event or comes from another place, the image is recycled. Check landmarks, signs, weather and shadows against the claimed location (geolocation) and time (chronolocation).
    3. Second, independent source. Call someone in a position to know who is independent of the first source: a hospital, police, the county disaster officer, the court registry. For allegations against a person, you must also seek their response (right of reply).
    A useful rule is the two-source rule: a contested or surprising factual claim needs two independent confirmations, or a single authoritative on-the-record source with direct knowledge, before broadcast. Each check costs time; on deadline, prioritise checks on the items most likely to be wrong and most damaging if wrong.

    Probability thinking

    Verification updates your confidence. If a tip channel is right 40% of the time and an independent official confirmation is wrong only 5% of the time, Bayes' theorem gives the confidence after confirmation:

    $$P(\text{true}\mid\text{conf}) = \frac{P(\text{conf}\mid\text{true})\,P(\text{true})}{P(\text{conf}\mid\text{true})P(\text{true}) + P(\text{conf}\mid\text{false})P(\text{false})}$$

    With $P(\text{conf}\mid\text{true}) = 0.9$ and $P(\text{conf}\mid\text{false}) = 0.05$: $\dfrac{0.9\times0.4}{0.9\times0.4 + 0.05\times0.6} = \dfrac{0.36}{0.39} \approx 0.92$. One good independent confirmation lifts a 40% tip to about 92%.

    Defamation

    Defamation is the publication of a false statement that damages a person's reputation in the eyes of right-thinking members of society. In Kenya it is a civil wrong governed by the Defamation Act (Cap. 36) and common law; broadcast defamation is treated as libel. The Constitution (Articles 33 and 34) protects freedom of expression and media freedom, but not defamation, hate speech or incitement.

    • Calling someone a "thief" or "corrupt" as a statement of fact, without proof you could defend in court, is the classic libel risk.
    • Defences include truth (justification), honest opinion on facts truly stated, and privilege, for example fair and accurate reports of court proceedings.
    • Court reporting: a person charged is accused, not guilty. Write "charged with assault; pleaded not guilty". Writing "MP guilty of assault" before a verdict is defamatory and may also prejudice the trial (contempt of court).
    • "Alleged" is not a magic word: repeating a defamatory allegation with "allegedly" attached is still publication if you cannot support it.

    Privacy and children

    The Media Council of Kenya Code of Conduct for the Practice of Journalism and the Children Act require special care with minors: do not identify children who are victims, missing in sensitive circumstances or involved in crime without the consent of a parent and a clear public interest. Respect grief: do not name casualties before families are informed.

    Right of reply

    If a story criticises a person or organisation, fairness requires that they are given a reasonable chance to respond before broadcast, and that their response is reported fairly. "They declined to comment" is acceptable only if they were truly asked in time.

    Accuracy and corrections

    Errors must be corrected promptly and with similar prominence. A newsroom that broadcasts a hoax death or a recycled disaster photo loses audience trust that takes years to rebuild.

    This is a general educational summary, not legal advice. Media law differs between countries; newsrooms keep legal advisers for difficult calls.

    The first hour

    In a crisis, information fills a vacuum. If the organisation says nothing, rumour, critics and journalists will define the story. Practitioners speak of a golden hour: aim to issue a short holding statement within about an hour, even if you know little. A holding statement acknowledges the situation, shows concern, says what you are doing and when you will update.

    Frameworks

    CAP: Concern, Action, Perspective

    Lead with genuine concern for those affected, then concrete actions, then perspective (facts that put the risk in context). Never lead with perspective: "only six children" sounds callous.

    CERC (CDC)

    Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action, show respect.

    SCCT (Coombs)

    Situational Crisis Communication Theory: match the response to responsibility. For a preventable crisis (a seal defect your plant caused), accommodative strategies (apology, corrective action, compensation) protect reputation; denial backfires.

    Stakeholder mapping

    Mendelow's power–interest grid: manage closely (high power, high interest: regulator, retailers, media), keep satisfied (high power, lower interest: board), keep informed (high interest, lower power: staff, farmers).

    Common mistakes the simulator punishes

    • Denial before the facts are known. If evidence later contradicts you, the story becomes your dishonesty.
    • Blaming victims or competitors. "Customers stored the milk badly" turns a product problem into a moral one.
    • "No comment". Audiences read it as guilt. Say what you can.
    • Legal threats against critics. Suing or threatening a person who posted a video spreads it further (the Streisand effect).
    • Broken promises. If you say "we will update at 4 pm", update at 4 pm.

    A simple sentiment model

    The simulator models public sentiment $S \in [-100, 100]$ as the balance between the heat of the story $h$ and the momentum $m$ your actions create, scaled by your credibility $c \in [0,1]$:

    $$\frac{dS}{dt} = -k\,h\,(1 - 0.5c) + m, \qquad \frac{dm}{dt} = -\frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}\,m$$

    Good statements add momentum in proportion to credibility. Momentum fades with a half-life $t_{1/2}$ of about 20 minutes of story time, which is why a single statement is not enough: you must keep updating.

    Worked example 1: rewriting a weak lede

    Draft: "Floods were experienced in Tana River where many people were displaced and Amina H., 9, is feared drowned." (19 words)

    1. Voice: "were experienced", "were displaced" are passive. Make the river the subject: "The Tana River burst its banks…"
    2. Specifics: "many people" becomes the attributed figure "about 1,800 households".
    3. 5Ws: Who (households, County Commissioner), What (burst banks, displaced), Where (Garsen, Tana River County), When (overnight).
    4. Attribution: "…the County Commissioner said."
    5. Privacy: remove the missing child's name. The Red Cross asked media not to publish it; "a nine-year-old girl is missing" carries the human interest without identifying her.

    Rewrite: "About 1,800 households have fled their homes in Garsen after the Tana River burst its banks overnight, the County Commissioner said on Monday." (24 words: passes length, active voice, 5Ws and attribution.)

    Worked example 2: does the bulletin fit?

    Candidate stories (default durations): flood 90 s, fuel 60 s, water main 45 s, MP in court 45 s, stadium 45 s, Kisumu protest 45 s.

    $$\sum d_i = 90 + 60 + 45 + 45 + 45 + 45 = 330\ \text{s} = 5{:}30$$

    That is 30 s over a 5:00 slot, outside the ±20 s tolerance. Options: trim the stadium to 30 s and the protest to 30 s (total 300 s), or drop the lowest-value story. Using the weighted model, the protest scores $W = C + P + T = 3$ and the stadium $W = Pr + T + P = 3$, so trimming both is fairer than dropping either.

    Worked example 3: the golden hour

    At T+0:00 a video alleges plastic in Maziwa Bora milk. By T+0:30 an influencer shares it. A holding statement at T+0:40 from the PR manager, on social media and the website, with concern + "testing samples" + "update by 3 pm":

    • Acknowledges without denying (safe: you do not yet know the cause).
    • Commits to an update time, which must then be kept.
    • Meets the golden hour, so journalists' first stories carry your side.

    When QA identifies the faulty seals at T+2:00, the company can recall batch B-2231, which is consistent with the holding statement. Had it said "our milk is 100% safe", the QA finding would expose the denial and credibility falls by about a third in the model.
