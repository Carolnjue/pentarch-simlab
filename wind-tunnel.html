Why it matters: every aircraft wing, wind-turbine blade and racing-car spoiler is an airfoil. A wind tunnel holds the model still and blows air past it, so engineers can measure lift and drag safely before anything flies. Sections 1, 4, 5 and 6 are the core ideas; sections 2 and 3 give the mathematical theory behind them and can be read later.

1. Bernoulli and the pressure coefficient

Along a streamline of steady, incompressible, inviscid flow the total pressure is constant:

$$p + \tfrac12\rho V^2 = p_\infty + \tfrac12\rho V_\infty^2 = p_0$$

Wherever the air speeds up, its static pressure drops. Engineers express the local pressure as a dimensionless pressure coefficient:

$$C_p = \frac{p - p_\infty}{\tfrac12\rho V_\infty^2} = 1 - \left(\frac{V}{V_\infty}\right)^2$$

At a stagnation point $V = 0$ so $C_p = 1$ (red in the colour map). Over the upper surface of a lifting airfoil the flow is faster than the free stream, so $C_p$ is negative (blue to cyan): that is suction. By convention $-C_p$ is plotted upwards, so the area between the upper and lower curves is proportional to the section lift coefficient.

$$c_l \approx \int_0^1 \left(C_{p,\text{lower}} - C_{p,\text{upper}}\right)\, d\!\left(\tfrac{x}{c}\right)$$

2. Potential flow, circulation and Kutta–Joukowski

For irrotational, incompressible flow we can write a complex potential $w(\zeta) = \phi + i\psi$. Lines of constant stream function $\psi$ are streamlines. Flow at speed $V_\infty$ and angle $\alpha$ past a circle of radius $R$ centred at $\zeta_0$, with circulation $\Gamma$, is

$$w(\zeta) = V_\infty\!\left[(\zeta-\zeta_0)e^{-i\alpha} + \frac{R^2 e^{i\alpha}}{\zeta-\zeta_0}\right] + \frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln(\zeta-\zeta_0)$$

The Joukowski transformation $z = \zeta + a^2/\zeta$ maps a circle passing through $\zeta = a$ into an airfoil with a sharp trailing edge at $z = 2a$. Shifting the circle centre left thickens the section; shifting it up adds camber. Because the map is conformal, streamlines stay streamlines, and the velocity in the airfoil plane is

$$u - iv = \frac{dw/d\zeta}{dz/d\zeta} = \frac{dw/d\zeta}{1 - a^2/\zeta^2}$$

Any value of $\Gamma$ gives a valid potential flow, but only one is physical. The Kutta condition says the flow must leave the sharp trailing edge smoothly. That puts the rear stagnation point exactly at the trailing edge and fixes

$$\Gamma = 4\pi V_\infty R \sin(\alpha + \beta), \qquad \beta = \arctan\frac{\text{Im}\,\zeta_0}{a - \text{Re}\,\zeta_0}$$

The Kutta–Joukowski theorem then gives the lift per unit span, $L' = \rho V_\infty \Gamma$. Try switching off the Kutta condition in your head: a circulation of zero would put the rear stagnation point on the upper surface and the flow would whip around the sharp edge at infinite speed. Real viscous flow cannot do that, which is why a starting vortex is shed and the circulation builds up.

The "equal transit time" myth. Switch on pulsed smoke timelines. Air going over the top arrives at the trailing edge well before the air going underneath: it does not "meet up again". Lift comes from the circulation that the Kutta condition demands, equivalently from the airfoil turning the flow downwards (Newton's third law). Bernoulli and Newton are two views of the same physics.

3. Thin-airfoil theory

Replacing the airfoil by a vortex sheet along its camber line $y_c(x)$ and enforcing flow tangency gives the classic results (with $x = \tfrac{c}{2}(1-\cos\theta)$):

$$c_l = 2\pi(\alpha - \alpha_{L=0}), \qquad \alpha_{L=0} = -\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\frac{dy_c}{dx}(\cos\theta - 1)\,d\theta$$
$$c_{m,c/4} = \frac{\pi}{4}(A_2 - A_1), \qquad A_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\frac{dy_c}{dx}\cos n\theta\,d\theta$$

A symmetric section has $\alpha_{L=0} = 0$ and $c_{m,c/4} = 0$. Camber shifts the lift curve to the left (lift at zero incidence) and adds a nose-down moment. The aerodynamic centre is at the quarter chord. Real sections reach about $0.1$ per degree, roughly 90% of $2\pi$ per radian, because the boundary layer slightly reduces the effective camber; this tunnel uses $a_0 = 0.9\times 2\pi$.

The Inviscid CL readout is the exact Joukowski (potential-flow) value for the thick, cambered section shown. Thickness raises the ideal lift slope above $2\pi$, by roughly $(1 + 0.77\,t/c)$, so this value is higher than the thin-airfoil result. Viscous effects then bring the real value down, which is what the balance shows.

The NACA 4-digit series defines the section by digits m p tt: maximum camber $m$ (% chord), its position $p$ (tenths of chord) and thickness $t$ (% chord). NACA 2412: 2% camber at 40% chord, 12% thick. The mean line is

$$y_c = \begin{cases}\dfrac{m}{p^2}\left(2px - x^2\right) & x \le p\\[2mm] \dfrac{m}{(1-p)^2}\left[(1-2p) + 2px - x^2\right] & x > p\end{cases}$$

4. From section to wing

The force on a model or wing of planform area $S = bc$ is

$$L = \tfrac12 \rho V^2 S\, C_L, \qquad D = \tfrac12 \rho V^2 S\, C_D, \qquad M = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S c\, C_m$$

A finite wing sheds trailing vortices from its tips. The downwash they induce tilts the lift backwards, creating induced drag, and reduces the lift slope. Prandtl's lifting-line theory gives, with aspect ratio $AR = b^2/S$ and span efficiency $e$:

$$C_{D} = c_{d} + \frac{C_L^2}{\pi e\,AR}, \qquad a = \frac{a_0}{1 + a_0/(\pi e AR)}$$

The familiar parabolic drag polar $C_D = C_{D0} + kC_L^2$ with $k = 1/(\pi e AR)$ follows. The maximum lift-to-drag ratio occurs where induced drag equals zero-lift drag: $C_L^* = \sqrt{C_{D0}/k}$ and $(L/D)_{max} = 1/(2\sqrt{k\,C_{D0}})$.

5. Reynolds number and the boundary layer

$$Re = \frac{\rho V c}{\mu}$$

$Re$ compares inertial to viscous forces. Close to the surface a thin boundary layer slows from the outer velocity to zero (no-slip). At low $Re$ it is laminar and has low skin friction but separates easily; above roughly $5\times10^5$ it becomes turbulent, with higher friction but much better resistance to an adverse pressure gradient. Here the skin-friction coefficient uses the Prandtl–Schlichting transitional formula $C_f = 0.455/(\log_{10}Re)^{2.58} - 1700/Re$, and $c_{d0} \approx 2C_f\,(1 + 2t + 60t^4)$.

6. Stall

Behind the suction peak the pressure rises towards the trailing edge (an adverse pressure gradient). The boundary layer loses momentum as it climbs this pressure hill. As $\alpha$ increases the suction peak grows, the boundary layer separates progressively from the trailing edge forward, and finally the flow breaks away over most of the upper surface. Lift falls, drag rises steeply and the pitching moment turns nose-down. Thin sections can stall abruptly from the leading edge; thicker ones stall more gently from the trailing edge. The tunnel models $c_{l,max}$ between about $0.8$ (flat plate) and $1.7$ (cambered 12% sections), rising slowly with Reynolds number.

7. The spinning cylinder (Magnus effect)

A spinning cylinder drags air round with it, creating circulation $\Gamma \approx 2\pi R^2\omega$ (an inviscid idealisation). With spin ratio $s = \omega R/V$ the ideal lift coefficient (based on diameter) is $C_L = 2\pi s$. When $s > 2$ the two stagnation points merge and lift off the surface. Real cylinders separate, so measured lift is much lower; this tunnel's balance uses an empirical curve. Flettner rotor ships and the curving free kick both rely on this effect.

What this tunnel computes. The streamlines, smoke and pressure colours come from an exact inviscid potential-flow solution: the flow past a circle, conformally mapped into a Joukowski airfoil with the same thickness and camber as your NACA section, with the Kutta condition fixing the circulation. Real air is viscous, so the force balance uses a calibrated model on top of thin-airfoil theory (lift slope, stall, skin-friction and pressure drag). Beyond the stall the potential-flow picture is no longer valid; the turbulent wake you see is a model, not a solution of the Navier–Stokes equations.
