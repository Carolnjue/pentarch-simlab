From sensor to screen

A water scheme like the one above is run from a SCADA system (Supervisory Control And Data Acquisition). It has four layers:

  1. Field instruments and actuators. Transmitters convert a process variable into a standard signal, usually a 4–20 mA current loop. Actuators (motor starters, variable-speed drives, valve actuators) do the physical work.
  2. PLC (Programmable Logic Controller). It runs a scan cycle every few tens of milliseconds: read inputs, execute logic (interlocks, sequences, PID loops), write outputs. Control happens here, not on the screen. If the SCADA PC fails, the PLC keeps the plant running.
  3. SCADA server and HMI (Human–Machine Interface). Polls the PLCs, draws the graphics, raises alarms and sends operator commands back down.
  4. Historian. Stores time-stamped values so you can trend and investigate after an event.

Scaling a 4–20 mA signal

The live zero (4 mA rather than 0 mA) lets the PLC tell "zero level" apart from "broken wire". A transmitter is calibrated over a range from its lower range value (LRV, the reading at 4 mA) to its upper range value (URV, at 20 mA); in between the current is proportional to the process variable (PV, the quantity being measured):

$$\text{PV} = \text{LRV} + \frac{I-4\ \text{mA}}{16\ \text{mA}}\,(\text{URV}-\text{LRV})$$

LT-401 is ranged 0–4.5 m, so 13.6 mA means $0 + \tfrac{9.6}{16}\times 4.5 = 2.70$ m. Under the NAMUR NE 43 convention a current below 3.6 mA or above 21 mA signals a fault, and the HMI should show the value as bad quality. A transmitter that is stuck at a plausible value is harder: its quality looks fine, and only comparison with other evidence (a second transmitter, a mass balance, the absence of normal signal noise) reveals it.

Control modes, permissives and interlocks

  • Auto / Manual (Hand–Off–Auto). In Auto the PLC starts and stops equipment. In Manual the operator commands it from the faceplate. Start/Stop buttons are disabled in Auto so that the operator and the PLC never fight over the same device.
  • Permissive: a condition that must be true before something may start (BP-101 cannot start unless XV-101 is open).
  • Interlock / trip: a condition that forces equipment off (booster pumps stop on reservoir low-low level LSL-403; a motor trips on thermistor over-temperature or thermal overload). Trips latch and need a deliberate reset.
  • Sequence: a timed series of steps (the filter backwash: drain, air scour, water wash, settle).

The reservoir level loop

The reservoir obeys a simple mass balance. With plan area $A$ = 180 m²:

$$A\frac{dh}{dt} = Q_\text{in} - Q_\text{out} - Q_\text{backwash} - Q_\text{overflow}$$

LIC-401 is a PI (proportional–integral) controller that moves inlet valve LCV-401 to hold the level at its setpoint (the target level). In words: the P part opens the valve more the further the level is below target; the I part keeps adding opening for as long as any error remains, so the level ends up exactly on target. $e$ is the error, $u$ the valve opening:

$$u(t) = K_p\,e(t) + \frac{K_p}{T_i}\int_0^t e\,d\tau,\qquad e = h_\text{SP} - h_\text{measured}$$

Here $K_p$ = 120 %/m and $T_i$ = 25 min, with anti-windup (the integral stops when the valve is fully open or shut). The borehole pump runs while LCV-401 is open more than 10 % and goes to "sleep" after the valve has been below 5 % for a minute. Note the weakness: the controller trusts the measured level. If LT-401 freezes low, the controller keeps opening the valve. That is why independent float switches (LSHH-402, LSL-403) exist and why the backup transmitter LT-402 is compared continuously.

This is a teaching model. Hydraulics use quadratic pump and system curves solved every simulation step, a linear well drawdown model, and well-mixed tanks for chlorine. Real plants add many more details (water hammer, motor starting currents, chlorine decay kinetics that depend on temperature and organic matter), but the cause-and-effect chains here are the ones operators really use.

Pump curve meets system curve

A centrifugal pump's head falls as flow rises. A good approximation is a parabola; the affinity laws scale it with speed ratio $r = n/n_0$ (for a VFD, $r = f/50$ Hz):

$$H_p(Q) = H_0 r^2 - aQ^2,\qquad Q \propto r,\quad H \propto r^2,\quad P \propto r^3$$

The pipework needs a head that rises with flow: a static lift plus friction. For a borehole the static lift itself grows with flow because the water level draws down:

$$H_\text{sys}(Q) = \underbrace{\Big(h_\text{SWL} + \frac{Q}{S_c}\Big)}_{\text{pumping level}} + z + kQ^2 + \Delta h_\text{filter}(Q) + \Delta h_\text{valves}(Q)$$

where $S_c$ is the specific capacity (m³/h per metre of drawdown). The pump runs where the curves cross. Watch it live below: change the VFD speed, throttle LCV-401 or let the filter clog, and the operating point moves.

Dry-running versus cavitation

Cavitation happens when the pressure at the impeller eye falls below the vapour pressure, so vapour bubbles form and collapse violently. The criterion is net positive suction head:

$$\text{NPSH}_a = \frac{p_\text{atm} - p_v}{\rho g} + h_\text{sub} - h_\text{loss} \;>\; \text{NPSH}_r + \text{margin}$$

At about 1,600 m altitude in central Kenya, $p_\text{atm} \approx 84$ kPa, and at 25 °C $p_v \approx 3.2$ kPa, so the atmospheric term is about 8.2 m. A submersible pump with 20 m of submergence has an NPSHa near 28 m, far above a typical NPSHr of 3–6 m. Submersibles rarely cavitate. Their danger is dry-running: if the pumping level falls to the intake, the pump draws air, flow and motor current collapse, and the motor (which is cooled by water flowing past it) overheats within minutes.

The signature of dry-running: motor current falls well below normal (around 40 % of full-load current), flow falls to near zero, wellhead pressure drops, well level is at the intake, and motor temperature rises. A closed discharge valve (dead-heading) also gives low current and low flow, but the pressure is high, not low. Compare tags before you act.

Sustainable yield

With a linear drawdown model the maximum flow that keeps a submergence margin $m$ above the intake at depth $z_i$ is

$$Q_\text{max} = S_c\,(z_i - m - h_\text{SWL})$$

When a drought lowers the static level and screen encrustation reduces $S_c$, $Q_\text{max}$ can drop below the pump's 50 Hz output. The fix is to reduce speed (the affinity laws), install a low-level probe or underload relay that trips the pump, and plan rehabilitation of the borehole.

What an alarm is for

An alarm is an audible and/or visible means of telling the operator about an equipment malfunction, process deviation or abnormal condition that requires a timely response (ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682). If no action is needed, it should be an event or a message, not an alarm.

Priority is coded redundantly by colour, shape and number, so that it is still readable by colour-blind operators and on a monochrome display.

The alarm state model

  1. Unacknowledged, active: flashing, horn sounding. The operator has not yet seen it.
  2. Acknowledged, active: steady. The operator has seen it; the condition still exists.
  3. Unacknowledged, returned to normal: the condition cleared before anyone looked. It stays listed so it is not missed.
  4. Shelved: temporarily hidden by the operator (here for one hour of plant time), for example a known nuisance alarm during maintenance. Shelving must be tracked and time-limited because a shelved alarm cannot warn you.

Acknowledging is not fixing

Pressing "Ack" only silences the horn and stops the flashing. Good practice: acknowledge, assess, act, log. Read the whole alarm list, look for the first alarm in the sequence (it often points at the root cause), confirm with trends and other tags, act, and write it down.

Alarm floods and standing alarms

EEMUA 191 suggests an average of no more than about one alarm per 10 minutes per operator in normal operation, and calls more than 10 alarms in 10 minutes a flood. A power failure easily produces a flood, which is why the first alarm matters. Standing alarms (active for days) and shelved alarms train operators to ignore them. In the power-failure exercise, an ignored low-battery or low-fuel alarm from the previous shift is exactly what turns a short interruption into a long outage.

Rationalisation tools

  • Deadband: the value must return past the limit by a margin before the alarm clears, to stop chattering.
  • On-delay: the condition must persist for a set time (here 10–60 s) before the alarm is raised.
  • State-based suppression: low-flow alarms on a pump are only meaningful while the pump is commanded to run.

Why chlorinate

Free chlorine (hypochlorous acid HOCl and hypochlorite OCl⁻) inactivates bacteria and viruses and leaves a residual that protects water in the pipes. Sodium hypochlorite dissociates as

$$\mathrm{NaOCl + H_2O \rightarrow HOCl + NaOH},\qquad \mathrm{HOCl \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OCl^-}\ (\text{p}K_a \approx 7.5)$$

HOCl is roughly 80 times more effective than OCl⁻, which is why disinfection works best at pH below 8.

Dose, demand and residual

$$C_\text{residual} = C_\text{dose} - C_\text{demand}$$

The demand is the chlorine consumed by iron, manganese, ammonia and organic matter. Turbid water carries more demand and shields microbes, so filtration first, then disinfection. The plant doses flow-paced: mass rate $\dot m = C_\text{dose}\,Q$. At 1.8 mg/L and 65 m³/h that is 117 g/h of chlorine, about 0.94 L/h of 12.5 % hypochlorite.

The CT concept

Inactivation depends on the product of residual concentration and contact time:

$$CT = C \times T_{10}\quad[\text{mg}\cdot\text{min/L}],\qquad T_{10} = \text{BF}\times\frac{V}{Q}$$

$T_{10}$ is the time by which only 10 % of the water has passed through the tank, found from the hydraulic residence time $V/Q$ multiplied by a baffling factor BF (0.1 for an unbaffled tank, 0.3 for a poorly baffled reservoir, 0.7 or more for a serpentine contact tank, 1.0 for a pipe). WHO guidance for effective disinfection is a free residual of at least 0.5 mg/L after at least 30 min contact at pH below 8, that is CT ≥ 15 mg·min/L. At the consumer's tap, aim for 0.2–0.5 mg/L.

Model used in the simulator

The contact pipe is two well-mixed cells in series (a simple stand-in for plug flow) and the reservoir is one well-mixed tank with first-order bulk decay $k_b$:

$$V\frac{dC}{dt} = Q_\text{in}\,C_\text{in} - Q_\text{in}\,C - k_b V C$$

At steady state $C = C_\text{in}\,\dfrac{Q}{Q + k_b V}$. With $k_b$ = 0.025 h⁻¹, a 600 m³ volume and 60 m³/h inflow, $C = 0.8\,C_\text{in}$. Because the reservoir is large, its outlet residual (AIT-302) changes slowly. The contact analyser AIT-301 is your early warning of a dosing failure.

Overdosing is also a quality failure: above about 2–3 mg/L customers complain of taste and odour, and WHO's health-based guideline value for chlorine is 5 mg/L. Never run both dosing pumps at the full flow-paced dose at the same time.

1. Will the borehole run dry?

During a drought the static water level is 46 m below ground and the specific capacity has fallen to $S_c$ = 0.85 m³/h per m. The intake is at 88 m. What is the largest flow that keeps 3 m of submergence?

$Q_\text{max} = S_c(z_i - m - h_\text{SWL}) = 0.85\times(88 - 3 - 46) = 0.85 \times 39 = 33$ m³/h.

At 50 Hz BP-101 wants about 60 m³/h, which would need a pumping level of $46 + 60/0.85 = 117$ m, below the intake. It will dry-run. In the simulator the pump settles at about 32 m³/h with 4–5 m of submergence at 43 Hz, and about 30 m³/h with 7 m of submergence at 42 Hz, the setting SOP-01 recommends.

Affinity check: flow falls faster than speed here because the static head (pumping level) is large; at 42 Hz the shut-off head is $160 \times 0.84^2 = 113$ m.

2. Reservoir time-to-empty

The raw main bursts at 10:00. The reservoir is at 2.9 m and the town draws 60 m³/h. Booster pumps cut out at 0.4 m (LSL-403). How long does storage last?

Usable volume $= A\,\Delta h = 180 \times (2.9 - 0.4) = 450$ m³. Time $= 450 / 60 = 7.5$ h. This is your window to isolate and repair the main. If the evening peak arrives (about 90 m³/h) the window shrinks. Calm, deliberate isolation is better than restarting the pump into a burst main.

3. Pump power and current

BP-101 delivers 65 m³/h against a total head of 112 m (65 m pumping level plus 47 m at the wellhead). Hydraulic power:

$P_h = \rho g Q H = 1000 \times 9.81 \times \frac{65}{3600} \times 112 = 19.8$ kW. With a pump efficiency of about 0.69, shaft power is about 28.8 kW.

The model's current is $I = \sqrt{I_m^2 + (k P)^2}$ with magnetising current $I_m = 0.35 \times 64 = 22.4$ A and $k = 1.91$ A/kW: $I = \sqrt{22.4^2 + 55^2} \approx 59$ A, about 92 % of the 64 A full-load current. Dry-running drops shaft power to about 5 kW, so $I \approx \sqrt{22.4^2 + 9.6^2} \approx 24$ A: the low-current alarm fires below 50 % (32 A). A throttled pump at 20 m³/h still draws about 40 A, so the alarm does not chatter during normal level control.

4. Hypochlorite consumption

Dose 1.8 mg/L = 1.8 g/m³. At 65 m³/h: $1.8 \times 65 = 117$ g/h of available chlorine. A 12.5 % (w/v) solution contains 125 g/L, so the pump delivers $117/125 = 0.94$ L/h, about 22 L/day. The 500 L day tank lasts about three weeks.

5. CT at the reservoir

Reservoir at 3.0 m holds $180 \times 3.0 = 540$ m³; outflow 50 m³/h. HRT $= 540/50 = 10.8$ h $= 648$ min. With BF = 0.3, $T_{10} = 194$ min. With 0.8 mg/L residual, $CT = 0.8 \times 194 = 155$ mg·min/L, ten times the 15 mg·min/L target. The contact pipe alone (30 m³ at 65 m³/h, BF = 1) gives $T_{10} = 28$ min and $CT = 1.2 \times 28 = 33$ mg·min/L.

6. Generator load

On the 80 kW generator: BP-101 about 29 kW of shaft power (31 kW electrical at 93 % motor efficiency), one booster about 19 kW (20 kW electrical), auxiliaries 3 kW: total about 54 kW, 68 % load. Starting the second booster adds 20 kW (74 kW, 93 %), and at peak flows the pumps draw more. Run one booster only on the generator.

Guided experiments

  1. Affinity laws. In free operation, open the BP-101 faceplate, switch to Manual and step the VFD from 50 to 40 Hz. Watch FT-101, the current and the pump-curve chart in the Pumps tab. Is $Q \propto f$? Explain why flow falls faster than speed (think about static head).
  2. Mass balance. Put LIC-401 in Manual with the valve at 100 %. Use the trend to measure $dh/dt$ and check $A\,dh/dt = Q_\text{in} - Q_\text{out}$ with FT-201 and FT-202.
  3. Controller tuning. Change the level setpoint from 3.2 to 2.6 m in Auto and watch how LCV-401 and the level respond. What would a larger proportional gain do?
  4. Chlorine lag. Stop the duty dosing pump in free operation and time how long AIT-301 and AIT-302 take to fall below 0.5 mg/L. Relate this to the residence times $V/Q$.
  5. Filter life. Run at 120× and watch DPIT-201 climb over a day. Backwash, and compare the filtered turbidity before and after.
