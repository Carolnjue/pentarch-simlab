Shafts and meshes

1. Pitch circles, module and the basic law

Why it matters: a motor spins fast but weakly; gears let you trade that speed for turning force (torque), or the reverse. A small gear driving a big one turns it slower and harder. Throughout, $z$ is the number of teeth, $\omega$ (omega) the angular speed in rad/s, $n$ the speed in rpm and $r$ a radius.

Two meshing spur gears behave like two cylinders rolling on each other without slip. Those imaginary cylinders are the pitch circles. Because the pitch circles roll together, the speed of a point on each pitch circle (the pitch-line velocity) is the same:

$$v = \omega_1 r_1 = \omega_2 r_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{z_2}{z_1}$$

Tooth size is set by the module $m$ (in mm). Gears can only mesh if they have the same module (and pressure angle). The pitch diameter, circular pitch and standard tooth heights are

$$d = m\,z, \qquad p = \pi m, \qquad h_a = m,\quad h_f = 1.25\,m,\quad d_a = m(z+2),\quad d_f = m(z-2.5)$$

The centre distance of an external pair is the sum of the pitch radii; for an internal (ring) pair it is the difference:

$$a = \frac{m(z_1+z_2)}{2} \qquad a_{\text{int}} = \frac{m(z_r - z_p)}{2}$$

2. Gear ratio, direction and idlers

We define the gear ratio as input speed over output speed, $i = n_{in}/n_{out}$. For a single external mesh $i = -z_2/z_1$; the minus sign records that external gears reverse the direction of rotation. In a simple train every gear sits on its own shaft:

$$i = \left(-\frac{z_2}{z_1}\right)\left(-\frac{z_3}{z_2}\right)\cdots = (-1)^{k}\,\frac{z_{\text{last}}}{z_{\text{first}}}$$

where $k$ is the number of meshes. The intermediate teeth cancel, so an idler gear changes the direction and the centre distance but not the magnitude of the ratio.

Try it: load the Idler preset, then change the idler's tooth count. The output speed does not move, only the spacing and the arrows.

3. Compound trains

To get large ratios without enormous gears, two gears of different size are fixed to the same shaft (a compound gear). They share one angular velocity, so the ratios of the stages multiply:

$$i = \frac{\text{product of driven teeth}}{\text{product of driving teeth}}\times(-1)^{k} = \frac{z_2\,z_4\,z_6\cdots}{z_1\,z_3\,z_5\cdots}(-1)^k$$

Each stage may use a different module, because only gears that actually mesh must match. Later stages carry more torque, so they are usually given a larger module (bigger, stronger teeth).

4. Torque, power and efficiency

Power is torque times angular velocity, $P = T\omega$ with $\omega = 2\pi n/60$ (n in rpm). An ideal gear train conserves power, so torque is multiplied by exactly the factor that speed is divided by. Real spur meshes lose roughly 1–2% each (tooth friction, plus oil churning at speed), so with $k$ meshes in series, each passing on a fraction $\eta$ (eta) of the power:

$$P_{out} = \eta^{k} P_{in}, \qquad T_{out} = \eta^{k}\,|i|\,T_{in}, \qquad n_{out} = \frac{n_{in}}{|i|}$$

This is why gearboxes are used: a small fast motor delivers little torque, and a reduction trades speed for torque at almost constant power.

5. The involute profile and the 20° pressure angle

Tooth flanks are involutes of a circle: the curve traced by the end of a taut string unwound from the base circle, of radius $r_b = r\cos\alpha$. In polar form, at radius $\rho$ the involute has turned through

$$\operatorname{inv}\alpha_\rho = \tan\alpha_\rho - \alpha_\rho,\qquad \cos\alpha_\rho = \frac{r_b}{\rho}$$

Because the normal to an involute is always tangent to the base circle, the contact force between two involute teeth always acts along one fixed line, the line of action, tangent to both base circles and inclined at the pressure angle $\alpha$ (standard: 20°) to the common tangent. Consequences: the velocity ratio is exactly constant (the law of gearing holds), and small errors in centre distance do not change the ratio.

The force at the mesh splits into a tangential part that transmits torque and a radial part that pushes the shafts apart:

$$F_t = \frac{T}{r},\qquad F_r = F_t\tan\alpha,\qquad F_n = \frac{F_t}{\cos\alpha}$$

6. Contact ratio

For smooth, continuous drive, the next pair of teeth must engage before the current pair leaves contact. The contact ratio is the length of the path of contact divided by the base pitch $p_b = \pi m\cos\alpha$:

$$\varepsilon_\alpha = \frac{\sqrt{r_{a1}^2-r_{b1}^2}+\sqrt{r_{a2}^2-r_{b2}^2}-a\sin\alpha}{\pi m\cos\alpha}$$

It should be at least about 1.2; typical spur gears achieve 1.4–1.8. Turn on Action in the toolbar to see the line of action, with the active path of contact drawn bold. Very small pinions (fewer than 17 teeth at 20°) suffer undercut: the cutting tool removes material at the tooth root, weakening it. The drawing here shows simplified undercut.

7. Planetary (epicyclic) gearsets and the Willis equation

A sun gear (s), planets carried on a carrier (c) and an internal ring gear (r). Looking from the carrier, it is an ordinary train with the carrier fixed, sun to ring through idler planets: $i_{\text{carrier fixed}} = -z_r/z_s$. Adding the carrier's motion back gives the Willis equation:

$$\frac{\omega_s-\omega_c}{\omega_r-\omega_c} = -\frac{z_r}{z_s}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad z_s\,\omega_s + z_r\,\omega_r = (z_s+z_r)\,\omega_c$$

Geometric conditions: equal module everywhere, $z_r = z_s + 2z_p$ (coaxial), and for $N$ equally spaced planets the assembly condition $(z_s+z_r)/N$ must be an integer. Torque balance on the three members: $T_s + T_c + T_r = 0$ (ideal), so the held member must react a torque.

8. Rack and pinion, worm and wheel

A rack is a gear of infinite radius. The rack moves at the pitch-line velocity $v = \omega r = \omega m z/2$, with force $F = T/r$. A worm is a screw with $z_1$ starts; one revolution advances the wheel by $z_1$ teeth, so $i = z_2/z_1$, giving 20–80:1 in one stage. The lead angle $\gamma$ and efficiency (worm driving, normal pressure angle $\alpha_n$) are

$$\tan\gamma = \frac{z_1 m}{d_1} = \frac{z_1}{q},\qquad \eta = \frac{\tan\gamma\,(\cos\alpha_n-\mu\tan\gamma)}{\cos\alpha_n\tan\gamma+\mu}$$

If $\mu \ge \cos\alpha_n\tan\gamma$ the wheel cannot drive the worm backwards: the drive is self-locking, useful for hoists and gates, at the price of low efficiency.

Model limits. The simulator uses standard full-depth 20° involute spur gears with zero profile shift and exact standard centre distances. Efficiency is a constant per mesh (in the planetary mode, applied twice). Tooth strength, bearing losses and dynamic loads are not modelled.

Example 1: two-stage compound reducer (the default build)

Motor: 1500 rpm, 2.0 N·m. Stage 1: $z_1 = 20$ drives $z_2 = 40$, module 2 mm. Stage 2 (on the same shaft as $z_2$): $z_3 = 15$ drives $z_4 = 45$, module 3 mm. Efficiency 0.98 per mesh.

  1. Ratio: $i = (-40/20)(-45/15) = (+2)(3) = 6$. Two meshes, so the output turns the same way as the motor.
  2. Output speed: $n_{out} = 1500/6 = 250$ rpm. Intermediate shaft: $1500/2 = 750$ rpm (opposite direction).
  3. Input power: $\omega_{in} = 2\pi(1500)/60 = 157.08$ rad/s, $P_{in} = 2.0\times157.08 = 314.2$ W.
  4. Output power: $P_{out} = 0.98^2\times314.2 = 301.7$ W. Output torque: $T_{out} = 0.98^2\times6\times2.0 = 11.52$ N·m (check: $301.7/26.18 = 11.52$).
  5. Pitch diameters: $d_1 = 2\times20 = 40$ mm, $d_2 = 80$ mm, $d_3 = 3\times15 = 45$ mm, $d_4 = 135$ mm. Centre distances: $a_{12} = 60$ mm, $a_{34} = 90$ mm.
  6. Pitch-line velocity, stage 1: $v = 157.08\times0.020 = 3.14$ m/s; stage 2: $78.54\times0.0225 = 1.77$ m/s.

Example 2: contact ratio of the 20/40 pair

$m = 2$ mm, $\alpha = 20°$. Radii: $r_1 = 20$, $r_{a1} = 22$, $r_{b1} = 20\cos20° = 18.794$ mm; $r_2 = 40$, $r_{a2} = 42$, $r_{b2} = 37.588$ mm; $a = 60$ mm.

  1. $\sqrt{22^2-18.794^2} = 11.436$ mm and $\sqrt{42^2-37.588^2} = 18.739$ mm.
  2. $a\sin\alpha = 60\times0.34202 = 20.521$ mm. Path of contact $= 11.436+18.739-20.521 = 9.654$ mm.
  3. Base pitch $p_b = \pi\times2\times\cos20° = 5.904$ mm, so $\varepsilon_\alpha = 9.654/5.904 = 1.64$. On average 1.64 tooth pairs share the load.
  4. Tooth force at the motor: $F_t = T/r_1 = 2.0/0.020 = 100$ N, $F_r = 100\tan20° = 36.4$ N.

Example 3: planetary reducer (the default planetary set)

$m = 2$ mm, $z_s = 24$, $z_p = 18$, so $z_r = 24+36 = 60$. Three planets: $(24+60)/3 = 28$, an integer, so they can be assembled equally spaced. Ring held, sun driven at 1500 rpm.

  1. Willis with $\omega_r = 0$: $\dfrac{\omega_s-\omega_c}{0-\omega_c} = -\dfrac{60}{24}\Rightarrow \omega_s = \omega_c(1+2.5)$, so $i = 3.5$.
  2. Carrier speed: $1500/3.5 = 428.6$ rpm, same direction as the sun.
  3. Torque with $\eta=0.98$ per mesh (two meshes): $T_c = 0.98^2\times3.5\times2.0 = 6.72$ N·m. The ring must be held with $T_r = -(T_s+T_c) \approx -(2.0 + (-6.72))$: about 4.7 N·m of reaction (sign conventions in the results panel).
  4. Centre distance sun–planet: $a = 2(24+18)/2 = 42$ mm; ring pitch diameter $d_r = 120$ mm.

Example 4: worm drive for a gate

Single-start worm ($z_1 = 1$), wheel $z_2 = 40$, $m = 2$ mm, $q = 10$ ($d_1 = 20$ mm), $\mu = 0.05$, $\alpha_n = 20°$. Motor 1500 rpm, 2 N·m.

  1. Ratio $i = 40/1 = 40$, wheel speed $= 37.5$ rpm.
  2. Lead angle $\tan\gamma = 1/10 \Rightarrow \gamma = 5.71°$.
  3. Efficiency $\eta = \dfrac{0.1(0.9397 - 0.05\times0.1)}{0.9397\times0.1+0.05} = \dfrac{0.09347}{0.14397} = 0.649$.
  4. Wheel torque $T = 0.649\times40\times2 = 51.9$ N·m. Self-locking? $\cos\alpha_n\tan\gamma = 0.094 > 0.05$: no; with $\mu \ge 0.094$ it would lock.

Guided experiments

  1. Idlers. Load Idler. Change the idler from 15 to 60 teeth. Record output speed and direction. Explain using the cancellation of teeth in the ratio formula.
  2. Torque for speed. On the default compound train set η = 1. Confirm $P_{out} = P_{in}$ in the shaft table while torque rises six-fold. Then set η = 0.95 and calculate the heat generated in watts.
  3. Contact ratio. Use a 2-gear train. Reduce the pinion from 40 to 12 teeth and watch $\varepsilon$ in the mesh table. Turn on Action to see the path of contact shrink. At what tooth count does undercut start?
  4. Planetary modes. Keep $z_s=24$, $z_p=18$. Try all three choices of held member and verify each ratio against the table in Theory. Which configuration reverses the output?
  5. Self-locking. In worm mode, increase μ until the "self-locking" LED lights. Compare the efficiency at that point with the 2-start and 4-start worms.
  6. Module and size. Double the module of a stage. What happens to centre distance, pitch-line velocity and tooth force for the same torque?
