Frequency is the heartbeat of the grid

Every synchronous generator in an AC grid spins in step. In Kenya, as in most of Africa and Europe, the nominal frequency is 50 Hz: a two-pole machine turns at 3,000 rpm, a 28-pole hydro generator at about 214 rpm. Electricity is not stored in the wires, so at every instant

$$P_\text{generation} = P_\text{demand} + P_\text{losses} + \frac{dE_\text{kinetic}}{dt}$$

When demand exceeds generation, the shortfall is drawn from the kinetic energy of the rotating machines, they slow down, and frequency falls. Frequency is therefore a system-wide, real-time measure of the balance between supply and demand.

Inertia

Think of every generator as a heavy flywheel: the more spinning mass, the harder it is to slow down, so frequency changes more gently. The inertia constant $H$ of a machine is its stored kinetic energy at rated speed divided by its power rating $S_\text{rated}$ (in MVA). $J$ is the moment of inertia of the rotor and $\omega_0$ its rated angular speed. $H$ is in seconds: how long the stored energy alone could supply full output.

$$H = \frac{\tfrac12 J\omega_0^2}{S_\text{rated}}\quad[\text{s}]$$

Typical values: large steam and geothermal turbines 3–6 s, hydro 2–4 s, reciprocating diesel engines 1–2 s. Wind farms (behind power converters), solar PV, batteries and HVDC links contribute no natural inertia. The system's total stored energy is $E_\text{sys} = \sum H_i S_i$, shown in the frequency panel in GW·s. When the sun is high, many synchronous units are switched off, $E_\text{sys}$ falls, and the same disturbance causes a faster and deeper frequency drop.

The aggregated swing equation

In words: if mechanical power in ($P_m$) is more than electrical power out ($P_e$), the rotor speeds up; if less, it slows down. For one machine, Newton's second law for rotation in per-unit form (every quantity expressed as a fraction of the machine's rating) gives

$$\frac{2H}{\omega_0}\frac{d\omega}{dt} = P_m - P_e\quad(\text{per unit on the machine base})$$

Treating the whole grid as one equivalent rotating mass (all machines swing together, which is a good approximation for the first few seconds of a large event) and writing it in frequency:

$$\frac{d\,\Delta f}{dt} = \frac{f_0}{2E_\text{sys}}\Big(\sum_i P_{m,i} - P_L\big(1 + D\,\tfrac{\Delta f}{f_0}\big)\Big),\qquad E_\text{sys} = \sum_i H_i S_i$$

$D$ is the load-damping constant (motor loads draw less power at lower frequency; here $D = 1.5$, so a 1 % fall in frequency reduces demand by 1.5 %). The simulator integrates this every 50 ms of simulated time.

Rate of change of frequency

Immediately after losing $\Delta P$ of generation, before any governor has moved,

$$\text{ROCOF} = \left.\frac{df}{dt}\right|_{t=0^+} = -\frac{f_0\,\Delta P}{2E_\text{sys}}$$

The initial ROCOF depends only on the size of the loss and the inertia. Many protection relays (for example on embedded generators) trip at ROCOF of 1–2 Hz/s, which is why low-inertia systems worry grid operators.

The frequency nadir

Frequency keeps falling until primary response (governors, battery) plus load damping catch up with the loss. The lowest point is the nadir. A deeper nadir comes from: a bigger loss, less inertia, slower or smaller primary response. If the nadir reaches the first stage of under-frequency load shedding (here 49.0 Hz), customers are disconnected automatically to save the system.

Primary control: governor droop

A governor changes a unit's mechanical power in proportion to the frequency deviation. With droop $R$ (per unit, typically 4–5 %):

$$\Delta P_i = -\frac{1}{R_i}\,\frac{\Delta f}{f_0}\,P_{\max,i}$$

A 4 % droop means that a 4 % frequency change (2 Hz) would move the unit through its whole rating. Because every governed unit sees the same frequency, droop shares the response automatically, in proportion to rating, without any communication. Units with no headroom (running flat out) cannot respond upwards: this is why geothermal plants run at full output contribute almost nothing to primary response, and why reserve must be held on flexible units.

The combined frequency response characteristic (stiffness) is

$$\beta = \sum_i \frac{P_{\max,i}}{f_0 R_i} + \frac{D\,P_L}{f_0}\quad[\text{MW/Hz}],\qquad \Delta f_\text{ss} = -\frac{\Delta P}{\beta}$$

Primary control stops the fall but leaves a steady-state error $\Delta f_\text{ss}$.

Secondary control: AGC

Automatic Generation Control integrates the area control error and moves the setpoints of participating units until frequency is back at 50 Hz:

$$\text{ACE} = -\beta_\text{bias}\,\Delta f\ (+\ \text{interchange error}),\qquad \frac{dP_\text{AGC}}{dt} = \frac{\text{ACE}}{T_\text{AGC}}$$

Here $\beta_\text{bias}$ = 500 MW/Hz and $T_\text{AGC}$ = 30 s, acting on hydro units that have AGC enabled. Watch their setpoint sliders move after an event. Switch AGC off and you become the AGC.

Tertiary control: dispatch

Over minutes to hours, the control engineer restores reserves by committing and dispatching units, ideally in merit order. That is your main job in this simulator: AGC can only use the hydro it has been given, and the water is limited.

Economic dispatch

To meet demand at least cost, units are loaded in order of increasing short-run marginal cost (fuel and variable operation and maintenance, not the cost of building the plant). Unit commitment constraints

  • Minimum stable generation (Pmin): a unit cannot run below it once synchronised.
  • Ramp rates (MW/min) limit how fast output can follow a setpoint.
  • Start-up times: geothermal about an hour from warm here, diesel 10 min, GT 7 min, hydro 3–4 min. Peakers must be started before the peak.
  • Reserve: enough headroom (spinning reserve) to cover the loss of the largest single infeed (the N-1 principle).

Cost and emissions

$$C = \sum_i c_i P_i\ [\text{USD/h}],\qquad \text{CO}_2\ \text{intensity} = \frac{\sum_i e_i P_i}{\sum_i P_i}\ [\text{g/kWh}]$$

Variable renewables

Wind output follows the cube of wind speed (between cut-in and rated speed), so a modest fall in wind speed can halve output within minutes. The lakeshore wind farm here is large (310 MW, about 15 % of the evening peak) and sits at the end of one long 400 kV line. That line is therefore a single point of failure: lose it, and you lose the whole wind farm at once.

Solar PV follows the sun with a bell-shaped profile peaking around 12:30 and is cut by passing clouds within seconds. At the evening peak, solar is zero.

Why low inertia matters

Wind, solar, batteries and HVDC connect through power electronics. They supply energy but not rotating mass. At midday with strong sun and wind, the operator may run few synchronous machines, so $E_\text{sys}$ is small and ROCOF after a trip is high. Remedies:

  • Keep a minimum number of synchronous units online (an inertia floor), even if they are more expensive.
  • Fast frequency response from batteries, which can deliver full power in a fraction of a second.
  • Grid-forming inverters and synthetic inertia (not modelled here).
  • Synchronous condensers: machines that spin without producing active power.

Over-frequency response

Grid codes require wind and solar plants to reduce output when frequency rises above 50.2 Hz (limited frequency-sensitive mode, over-frequency). In the simulator they reduce by 40 % of their output per hertz above 50.2 Hz. They also stay connected down to 47.5 Hz (fault ride-through of frequency).

Curtailment

At night when demand is low, geothermal at minimum stable output plus strong wind can exceed demand. The setpoint slider on wind and solar is a curtailment cap. Curtailing free wind energy hurts, but geothermal cannot be cycled down quickly, and frequency would rise.

Under-frequency load shedding (UFLS)

UFLS is the grid's last automatic defence against collapse. Relays on distribution feeders disconnect blocks of customers when frequency falls through set thresholds:

Over-frequency works the other way: above 51.0 Hz, over-frequency protection disconnects generation one unit at a time (largest non-hydro first), and above 52 Hz the remaining machines trip on overspeed. Below 47.5 Hz, generator under-frequency protection trips the machines to protect turbine blades from resonance, and the system collapses. After shedding, the operator restores load in steps only when reserve allows, otherwise frequency drops again.

Line protection and cascades

The DC power-flow approximation gives line flows from bus angles: $P_{ij} = (\theta_i - \theta_j)/x_{ij}$, with $\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{P}$. When a line trips, its flow redistributes onto parallel paths in inverse proportion to their reactance. In this simulator a line above 100 % raises an alarm and trips after 15 minutes; above 125 % it trips after 60 s. One overload trip can overload the next line: a cascade. Re-dispatch generation on either side of the congested corridor to relieve it.

Black start

  1. Assess and sectionalise. After a blackout all line breakers are open. Confirm what is available.
  2. Start black-start units. Hydro units with their own diesel auxiliary supplies (Gitari and Kiamba on the Tana bus) can start with no external power.
  3. Pick up local load in small blocks (30 MW). Each block is a sudden load step on a small, low-inertia island. Keep frequency above 49 Hz after each step; wait for it to recover.
  4. Energise the transmission backbone one line at a time (click a line, close its breaker).
  5. Start units that need external power: geothermal plants need station supply for their auxiliaries; diesel and gas turbines need a live bus. The HVDC link needs a strong AC system (at least 700 MW of synchronous plant online) before it can operate.
  6. Restore remaining load and rebuild reserve. The exercise is complete when 90 % of demand is served.
Real restoration also manages voltage (energising long unloaded lines raises voltage through line charging), cold-load pickup and synchronising islands. These are simplified away here.

1. ROCOF after a unit trip

Online synchronous plant at 05:00 in free operation: five geothermal units totalling 650 MW (H = 4 s) and three hydro stations totalling 643 MW (H = 3 s). Using $S \approx P_{\max}/0.9$ and $E = H\,S$:

$E_\text{sys} = 4\ \text{s} \times 650/0.9 + 3\ \text{s} \times 643/0.9 = 2889 + 2143 = 5032$ MW·s $\approx 5.0$ GW·s, as the Inertia readout shows.

Press Trip largest unit: Kiriti V, generating 150 MW, trips. Its own stored energy leaves with it, so use the inertia that remains: $E_\text{sys} = 5032 - 4 \times 160/0.9 = 5032 - 711 = 4321$ MW·s. Then $\text{ROCOF} = -\dfrac{f_0\,\Delta P}{2E_\text{sys}} = -\dfrac{50 \times 150}{2 \times 4321} = -0.87$ Hz/s.

In the "Low-inertia midday" scenario (Kiriti IV and Kiamba out) the Inertia readout is about 4.4 GW·s (4359 MW·s) and the 300 MW interconnector trips. The link has no rotating mass, so $E_\text{sys}$ does not change: $\text{ROCOF} = -50 \times 300/(2 \times 4359) = -1.72$ Hz/s, twice the 05:00 value: the loss is twice as large and the inertia is no higher than after the morning trip. The ROCOF readout is averaged over about half a second, while governors and load damping are already acting, so it peaks lower (about −0.3 and −1.1 Hz/s) than these instantaneous values.

2. Steady-state frequency after primary response

Governors on 643 MW of hydro (R = 4 %): $643/(50 \times 0.04) = 322$ MW/Hz. Battery 100 MW at R = 0.5 %: $100/(50\times0.005) = 400$ MW/Hz, but it saturates at 100 MW. Each term is $\beta_i = P_{\text{rated}}/(f_0 R)$. Load damping at 1,800 MW with D = 1.5: $1.5 \times 1800/50 = 54$ MW/Hz.

Losing 150 MW: if the battery is not saturated, $\beta = 322 + 400 + 54 = 776$ MW/Hz, so $\Delta f_\text{ss} = -\Delta P/\beta = -150/776 = -0.19$ Hz, giving 49.81 Hz until AGC restores 50 Hz. Check the assumption: the battery supplies $400 \times 0.19 = 77$ MW, below its 100 MW limit. (Geothermal at full output adds nothing upward, and the small governor dead bands are ignored.)

3. Merit order and marginal price

Demand 2,000 MW at 19:30. Available: wind 150 (0 $/MWh), geothermal 620 at near full (6–7), hydro 749 (12–16), import 300 (65), diesel 200 (175–195), GT 60 (280).

Cumulative: 150 + 620 = 770; + 749 = 1,519; + 300 = 1,819; + 181 MW from Bandari diesel = 2,000. Marginal unit: Bandari at 175 $/MWh.

Hourly cost ≈ $620 \times 6.3 + 749 \times 13.4 + 300 \times 65 + 181 \times 175 \approx 3{,}900 + 10{,}000 + 19{,}500 + 31{,}700 = 65{,}100$ USD/h. Diesel supplies under a tenth of the energy but almost half the cost, and in a market every unit is paid the 175 $/MWh marginal price.

4. DC power flow on two parallel lines

Suswa to Nairobi has two circuits L2a and L2b, each $x$ = 0.05 pu, carrying 340 MW each (680 MW total). In the DC approximation each line carries $P = (\theta_a - \theta_b)/x$, so two equal reactances share the flow equally. L2a trips: if the parallel path through other buses has a much higher reactance, nearly all 680 MW moves to L2b, rated 600 MW: 113 %. That is an overload you must clear (for example by importing less on the HVDC and generating more from Tana hydro and coastal diesel) before protection trips the second circuit and splits the grid. In the corridor scenario the lines show 333 MW each before the trip and 666 MW (111 %) on L2b after it.

5. Battery energy

A 100 MW / 200 MWh battery at 50 % state of charge holds 100 MWh. Discharging at 100 MW it lasts 1 hour. Holding 100 MW for 10 minutes of fast frequency response uses $100 \times 10/60 = 16.7 \approx 17$ MWh, leaving 83 MWh: enough for several such events, or 50 more minutes at full power.

Guided experiments

Change one thing at a time and keep the hour, demand and other settings the same. For each run record the Inertia readout, ΔP, ROCOF, Last nadir and the frequency once it settles.

  1. Inertia and ROCOF. In free operation at 05:00, trip the largest unit and note ROCOF and nadir. Restart the scenario, stop one hydro station (inertia falls) and repeat. Compare with $\text{ROCOF} = -f_0\Delta P/2E_\text{sys}$. Expect: less inertia gives a steeper initial fall and a deeper nadir for the same loss.
  2. Droop. Turn off AGC and the battery governor, then trip a unit. Measure the steady-state frequency and compute $\beta$. Turn the hydro governors off one by one: what happens? Expect: frequency settles a few tenths of a hertz low, close to $\Delta f = -\Delta P/\beta$ (about 49.6 Hz for the 150 MW trip at 05:00); each governor removed shrinks $\beta$, so the offset and the nadir get worse until load shedding operates.
  3. Merit order. At 18:00, dispatch to meet the peak at minimum cost while keeping reserve above the largest infeed. Watch the marginal price. Expect: cheap geothermal and hydro run first and the dearest unit you need, usually diesel, sets the price.
  4. Curtailment. At 03:00 with strong wind, lower geothermal to its minimum and see whether you still need to curtail wind. Expect: geothermal cannot go below its minimum stable output, so when demand is low some wind may still have to be spilled.
  5. Cascade. Load the system to the evening peak, trip L2a and watch L2b. Clear the overload by re-dispatch before it trips. Expect: L2b jumps to about 110 % of its rating; cutting the import and raising generation on the far side of the corridor brings it below 100 %.
