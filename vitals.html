Reading a bedside monitor

  • ECG (green): usually lead II. Each beat shows a P wave (atrial depolarisation), QRS complex (ventricular depolarisation) and T wave (repolarisation). Heart rate is counted from R–R intervals. The ST segment should sit on the baseline; elevation in two contiguous leads with chest pain suggests ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).
  • SpO₂ plethysmograph (yellow): pulse oximetry compares red and infrared light absorption to estimate the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen. A small, flat pleth means poor peripheral perfusion (cold, shocked patient) and an unreliable reading.
  • Arterial pressure (red): a sharp upstroke, a peak (systolic), a dicrotic notch (aortic valve closure) and a diastolic run-off.
  • Respiration (white): chest impedance changes with breathing. Rate and depth matter: a slow, shallow trace is a warning sign.
$$\text{MAP}\approx \text{DBP}+\tfrac13(\text{SBP}-\text{DBP}),\qquad CO = HR\times SV,\qquad \text{Shock index}=\frac{HR}{SBP}$$

The body defends blood pressure: when stroke volume falls, heart rate and vascular resistance rise. That is why tachycardia and a rising respiratory rate often come before hypotension. A falling blood pressure is a late sign. A shock index above about 0.9–1.0 is concerning even when the SBP looks "normal".

The ABCDE approach

Assess and treat in order of what kills first: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability (consciousness, glucose, pupils), Exposure. Call for help early, treat problems as you find them, and reassess after every intervention.

Triage

Triage sorts patients by urgency, not by order of arrival. The South African Triage Scale (SATS), widely used in African emergency centres, combines a physiological Triage Early Warning Score (TEWS) (mobility, respiratory rate, heart rate, systolic BP, temperature, AVPU, trauma) with clinical discriminators (for example chest pain, active seizure, low glucose) that can raise the category. Target times to be seen: red immediately, orange within 10 minutes, yellow within 60 minutes, green within 4 hours. Under-triage (too low a category) risks harm; over-triage clogs resuscitation areas.

Alarm fatigue

Monitors alarm often, and most alarms are not clinically important. Staff can become desensitised. Setting sensible, patient-specific limits and responding to trends, not single numbers, makes alarms meaningful.

1. NEWS2 for the sepsis patient at the start

RR 24, SpO₂ 93% on air, SBP 89, HR 121, new confusion, temperature 39.2 °C.

  1. RR 24 → 2. SpO₂ 93 (scale 1) → 2. Air → 0.
  2. SBP 89 → 3. HR 121 → 2. Confusion (C) → 3. Temp 39.2 → 2.
  3. Total = 2+2+0+3+2+3+2 = 14: high risk, emergency response.

2. Shock index in haemorrhage

  1. The motorcyclist starts with HR 106, SBP 120: shock index = 106/120 = 0.88.
  2. Ten minutes later, uncontrolled: HR 137, SBP 87: index = 1.57. The SBP fell late; the index rose early.
  3. MAP at 87/57 ≈ 57 + (87−57)/3 = 67 mmHg.

3. Opioid toxicity and oxygen

  1. RR 7, SpO₂ 86% on air, responds to voice: NEWS2 = 3 (RR) + 3 (SpO₂) + 0 + … + 3 (V) = at least 9.
  2. After high-flow oxygen, SpO₂ 95%, RR still 7. NEWS2: RR 3 + SpO₂ 1 + oxygen 2 + V 3 = still ≥ 9. The saturation looks better but ventilation (and CO₂ clearance) has not improved. Treat the cause.

5. Pulse oximeter pitch

Many monitors lower the pitch of the pulse "beep" as SpO₂ falls, so you can hear desaturation without looking. In this simulator the tone is 300 Hz + 12 Hz per % above 70%: 98% → 636 Hz, 85% → 480 Hz.

Guided experiments

  1. Compensated shock. In free play, raise HR to 125 and keep SBP at 115. Note NEWS2 and the shock index. Now drop SBP to 88: what changes?
  2. Pleth and perfusion. Lower the SBP below 80 and watch the pleth trace shrink.
  3. Alarm limits. Set the SpO₂ low limit to 94 and the heart-rate high limit to 100. Turn on sound and push a value across a limit; compare the medium and high-priority alarm tones.
  4. STEMI. Set ST to +3 mm. Compare the shape with a normal ECG.
  5. AF. Select AF: no P waves and an irregularly irregular rhythm.
