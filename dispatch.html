Live metrics

The structured call

Emergency call-takers follow a structured interrogation protocol (systems such as MPDS, CBD or locally developed criteria-based dispatch). The order matters: location first, because if the line drops you can still send help.

  1. Answer promptly. International standards (e.g. NENA, NFPA 1225) aim for 90% of calls answered within 15 s and 95% within 20 s; many centres target 10 s.
  2. Address / location. Verify it: street, cross street, landmark, district. Read it back.
  3. Call-back number. So you can reconnect if the line drops.
  4. "Tell me exactly what happened." Let the caller describe the problem in their own words.
  5. Key questions for that complaint: Is the patient awake? Breathing normally? How many injured? Anyone trapped? Any hazards (fire, fuel, gas, wires, weapons)?
  6. Assign a priority and dispatch. Dispatch can happen in parallel with questioning as soon as the key facts are known.
  7. Pre-arrival instructions. Coach the caller: CPR, bleeding control, evacuation, staying safe.
  8. Stay on the line for critical calls until help arrives or the caller is safe.
Agonal breathing is not breathing. Occasional gasps, snoring or gurgling in an unresponsive person are a sign of cardiac arrest. Callers often say "he's breathing" when they hear gasps, so ask "Is he breathing normally?"

Targets vary between services; the ambulance standard of reaching the most critical patients in about 7–8 minutes comes from cardiac arrest survival data. Over-triage (calling a P3 a P1) wastes scarce units and exposes crews to the risks of blue-light driving; under-triage costs lives.

Why minutes matter

For witnessed cardiac arrest, survival to hospital discharge falls by roughly 7–10% for each minute without CPR or defibrillation, and by about 3–4% per minute when bystanders perform CPR (Larsen et al., 1993; Valenzuela et al., 1997). The simulator uses a simple linear model:

$$S(t) = \max\big(0,\; S_0 - r\,t\big),\qquad r = \begin{cases}10\%/\text{min} & \text{no CPR}\\ 3.5\%/\text{min} & \text{with CPR}\end{cases}$$

with $S_0 \approx 70\%$ if defibrillated immediately. Starting dispatcher-assisted CPR at minute 2 and arriving at minute 8 gives $S = 70 - 10(2) - 3.5(6) = 29\%$, against $70 - 10(8) = 0\%$ (clamped) with no CPR.

Matching resources to need

ALS ambulance
Advanced Life Support: paramedics, defibrillator, airway, drugs. For cardiac arrest, stroke, major trauma, choking, labour.
BLS ambulance
Basic Life Support: EMTs, oxygen, AED, splints, transport. For stable injuries and minor illness, and as the nearest first response (CPR, defibrillation, airway clearance) to an arrest or choking when no ALS unit is free.
Fire engine
Fire suppression, gas leaks, smoke, and often first responder with a defibrillator.
Rescue unit
Heavy rescue: cutting equipment for vehicle entrapment, water and rope rescue.
Police
Crime in progress, violence, traffic control and scene safety at collisions.

Utilisation and queueing

A fleet's utilisation is the fraction of time its units are committed:

$$\rho = \frac{\lambda\,\bar{T}_{\text{job}}}{c}$$

where $\lambda$ is the incident rate (per hour), $\bar T_{\text{job}}$ the mean time a unit is committed per incident (travel + scene + hospital + return, hours), and $c$ the number of units. As $\rho$ approaches 1 the chance that no suitable unit is free rises sharply (Erlang C), and response times explode. Planners usually keep ambulance utilisation well below 0.5–0.6 to protect coverage.

The nearest available unit is not always the right one: sending the only ALS unit in the east to a P3 fracture leaves the east uncovered for the next cardiac arrest. Good dispatchers think about coverage, not just the call in front of them.

Travel time

$$t_{\text{travel}} = \sum_{\text{road segments}} \frac{\ell_i}{v_i}$$

In the simulator each block is 500 m; minor roads are driven at 35 km/h under emergency conditions and arterial roads (highlighted) at 60 km/h. Units follow the fastest route found by Dijkstra's algorithm.

Dispatcher-assisted CPR (adults)

Current resuscitation guidelines (European Resuscitation Council 2021; AHA 2020; ILCOR) recommend that dispatchers recognise cardiac arrest quickly (unresponsive and not breathing normally) and coach untrained callers through compression-only CPR:

  1. Put the phone on speaker; kneel beside the person, who should be on their back on a firm surface.
  2. Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest (lower half of the breastbone), other hand on top.
  3. Push hard and fast: depth 5–6 cm for an adult, rate 100–120 per minute, allowing full recoil between compressions.
  4. Count aloud with the caller to keep the rhythm. Send someone for an AED if one is nearby.
  5. Do not stop until the ambulance crew takes over.
$$\text{interval} = \frac{60\ \text{s}}{110\ \text{min}^{-1}} \approx 0.55\ \text{s per compression}$$

In the simulator, tap the metronome button in time with your counting. The console reports your rate from the last eight taps; keep it between 100 and 120.

Choking (child over one year, adult)

If the person cannot cough, speak or breathe: up to 5 back blows between the shoulder blades, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts, alternating. If they become unresponsive, start CPR.

Severe bleeding

Firm direct pressure on the wound with a cloth; do not lift it to look; add more cloth on top if it soaks through; keep the person lying down and warm.

This is a learning summary. Take an accredited first-aid course (e.g. Kenya Red Cross, St John Ambulance) to learn CPR on a manikin.

Controlling the call

Callers in crisis may scream, give fragments or no address. Techniques taught to call-takers:

  • Repetitive persistence: repeat the same calm request with the same words: "I need the address. Tell me the address."
  • Use their name and give them a task: tasks reduce panic and buy time.
  • Lower your voice and slow down; callers tend to mirror your pace.
  • Reassure honestly: "Help is being arranged while we talk" (once it is).

In the simulator, a hysterical caller gives only vague location clues until you reassure them; then they can give an exact address.

Looking after yourself

Dispatchers face repeated exposure to distress and high cognitive load, with no closure on most calls. Rates of burnout and post-traumatic stress symptoms are significant. Healthy centres build in breaks, peer support, defusing after critical calls and access to counselling. Recognise your own stress signs: tunnel vision, irritability, sleeplessness.

Cognitive load

Working memory holds only a handful of items. Protocols, checklists and on-screen prompts exist so dispatchers do not have to remember everything under pressure: follow the structure and it carries you.

Worked example 1: travel time

An ALS ambulance at City General must reach an incident 3 blocks east along the arterial highway and then 2 blocks north on minor roads.

$$t = \frac{3 \times 0.5\ \text{km}}{60\ \text{km/h}} + \frac{2 \times 0.5\ \text{km}}{35\ \text{km/h}} = 0.025\ \text{h} + 0.0286\ \text{h} = 0.0536\ \text{h} \approx 3.2\ \text{min}$$

Add a typical 1 min turnout (crew to vehicle) and the response is about 4.2 minutes: inside the 8-minute P1 target. The simulator shows ETAs computed exactly this way.

Worked example 2: cardiac arrest survival

Call answered at $t=0$. You confirm the address and "not breathing normally" at 0:40 and start CPR coaching at 1:30 (sim). The ALS unit arrives at 7:00.

$$S = 70 - 10(1.5) - 3.5(7.0 - 1.5) = 70 - 15 - 19.25 = 35.75\%$$

Without CPR coaching: $S = 70 - 10(7.0) = 0\%$. Telephone CPR turns a near-zero chance of survival into roughly one in three, which is why it is the most valuable thing a dispatcher does.

Worked example 3: utilisation

Five ambulances; 6 medical incidents per hour; each commits a unit for about 45 minutes (travel, scene, hospital, return).

$$\rho = \frac{6 \times 0.75}{5} = 0.9$$

At 90% utilisation there is very often no free ambulance. Either add units, shorten hospital handovers, or send BLS units to stable patients so ALS units stay free.
