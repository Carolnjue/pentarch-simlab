ADME: what the body does to a drug

Pharmacokinetics (PK) describes how drug concentration changes with time as the body Absorbs, Distributes, Metabolises and Excretes it. Pharmacodynamics (PD) describes what the drug does to the body at a given concentration. The left panel of the lab shows ADME as moving particles: an oral dose enters the gut, passes the liver (first-pass metabolism) and reaches the blood; drug then distributes into tissues and is cleared by the kidneys (to urine) and liver (metabolites).

One-compartment model, IV bolus

Treat the body as one well-mixed volume, the apparent volume of distribution $V_d$ (L). Elimination is first-order: the rate is proportional to the amount present. With clearance $CL$ (L/h, the volume of plasma completely cleared of drug per unit time):

$$k=\frac{CL}{V_d},\qquad C(t)=\frac{D}{V_d}\,e^{-kt},\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{k}\approx\frac{0.693}{k}$$

On a log scale the curve is a straight line of slope $-k$. Each half-life the concentration halves; after about 4–5 half-lives more than 94–97% of a single dose has gone. $V_d$ is "apparent": a drug that binds strongly to tissue can have a $V_d$ of hundreds of litres, far more than body water (about 42 L in a 70 kg adult).

Area under the curve and bioavailability

$$\text{AUC}_{0\to\infty}=\int_0^\infty C\,dt=\frac{F\cdot D}{CL}$$

AUC measures total exposure. Bioavailability $F$ is the fraction of a dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged, $F = \text{AUC}_\text{oral}/\text{AUC}_\text{IV}$ for equal doses. Incomplete absorption and first-pass metabolism in gut wall and liver both lower $F$.

Oral dosing: first-order absorption (the Bateman function)

$$C(t)=\frac{F\,D\,k_a}{V_d\,(k_a-k)}\left(e^{-kt}-e^{-k_a t}\right),\qquad t_{\text{max}}=\frac{\ln(k_a/k)}{k_a-k}$$

Faster absorption (larger $k_a$) gives an earlier, higher peak; AUC does not change because it depends only on $F$, $D$ and $CL$.

IV infusion

At a constant rate $R_0$ (mg/h) the concentration rises towards a plateau $C_{ss}=R_0/CL$ as $C(t)=\frac{R_0}{CL}(1-e^{-kt})$. It takes about 3.3 half-lives to reach 90% of the plateau, whatever the rate. The plateau level depends on clearance only; the time to get there depends on half-life only.

Repeated dosing, accumulation and steady state

When a dose $D$ is repeated every $\tau$ hours before the previous dose has been eliminated, drug accumulates until the amount eliminated per interval equals the amount given. For IV bolus doses (one-compartment):

$$R_\text{ac}=\frac{1}{1-e^{-k\tau}},\qquad C_{ss,\max}=\frac{D}{V_d}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{-k\tau}},\qquad C_{ss,\min}=C_{ss,\max}\,e^{-k\tau}$$
$$C_{ss,\text{avg}}=\frac{\text{AUC}_\tau}{\tau}=\frac{F\cdot D}{CL\cdot\tau}$$

$C_{ss,\text{avg}}$ depends on the dosing rate $D/\tau$, not on how it is split. Smaller, more frequent doses give the same average with less peak-to-trough swing. The peak-to-trough ratio is $e^{k\tau}$, so to stay inside a window with $\text{MTC}/\text{MEC}=r$ you need $\tau < \ln r / k$.

Loading dose and maintenance dose

$$\text{LD}=\frac{C_\text{target}\cdot V_d}{F},\qquad \text{Maintenance dose rate}=\frac{C_\text{target}\cdot CL}{F}$$

A loading dose fills the volume of distribution at once, so a drug with a long half-life reaches its target immediately rather than after 4–5 half-lives. The loading dose depends on $V_d$; the maintenance dose depends on $CL$.

Two-compartment model

Many drugs first mix in a central compartment (blood and well-perfused organs, $V_1$) and then distribute more slowly into a peripheral compartment (muscle, fat, $V_2$) with inter-compartmental clearance $Q$. After an IV bolus the curve is bi-exponential:

$$C(t)=A\,e^{-\alpha t}+B\,e^{-\beta t},\qquad \alpha+\beta=k_{10}+k_{12}+k_{21},\quad \alpha\beta=k_{10}k_{21}$$

with $k_{10}=CL/V_1$, $k_{12}=Q/V_1$, $k_{21}=Q/V_2$. The steep early α (distribution) phase is followed by the slower β (terminal elimination) phase; the clinically relevant half-life is usually $t_{1/2,\beta}=\ln 2/\beta$.

Organ impairment

Total clearance is the sum of renal and non-renal clearance. In this lab $CL = CL_\text{normal}\,[f_e\cdot(\text{renal function}) + (1-f_e)\cdot(\text{hepatic function})]$, where $f_e$ is the fraction normally excreted unchanged in urine. When clearance falls, $k$ falls, $t_{1/2}$ lengthens, accumulation increases and $C_{ss,\text{avg}}$ rises in proportion. Typical responses are to lower the dose, lengthen the interval, or both, and to monitor levels. (Real dose adjustment uses measured renal function such as creatinine clearance or eGFR and drug-specific guidance.)

The lab solves the linear compartment equations numerically (Runge–Kutta, step 0.01 h) and superposes single-dose responses to find the true steady state, so you can check every formula above against the curve.

Pharmacodynamics: the Emax model and the Hill equation

$$E=\frac{E_{\max}\,C^{\,n}}{EC_{50}^{\,n}+C^{\,n}}$$

$EC_{50}$ is the concentration giving half the maximum effect: a measure of potency (lower $EC_{50}$ = more potent). $E_{\max}$ is efficacy, the largest effect the drug can produce. The Hill coefficient $n$ sets steepness. On a linear axis the curve is a rectangular hyperbola (for $n=1$); on a log axis it becomes the familiar sigmoid, linear between about 20% and 80% of $E_{\max}$.

  • Full agonist: $E_{\max}$ = system maximum. Partial agonist: lower $E_{\max}$ even at full receptor occupancy.
  • Competitive (surmountable) antagonist shifts the curve to the right in parallel. The dose ratio is given by the Schild equation $DR = 1 + [I]/K_i$; $E_{\max}$ is unchanged because enough agonist outcompetes it.
  • Non-competitive (insurmountable) antagonist lowers $E_{\max}$; in the simplest case $E_{\max}' = E_{\max}/(1+[I]/K_i)$ and $EC_{50}$ is unchanged.

Quantal dose–response and therapeutic index

A quantal curve plots the percentage of a population showing an all-or-none response (therapeutic benefit, toxicity) against dose. The therapeutic index is $TI = TD_{50}/ED_{50}$ (or $LD_{50}/ED_{50}$ in animals). Because curves differ in slope, a stricter measure is the certain safety factor $TD_1/ED_{99}$. Drugs with a narrow TI (e.g. digoxin, lithium, aminoglycosides, warfarin) need careful dosing and often therapeutic drug monitoring.

1. Single IV bolus of teaching drug T1

T1: $V_d = 35$ L, $CL = 5$ L/h, dose 200 mg IV.

  1. $k = CL/V_d = 5/35 = 0.1429\ \text{h}^{-1}$
  2. $t_{1/2} = 0.693/0.1429 = 4.85$ h
  3. $C_0 = D/V_d = 200/35 = 5.71$ mg/L
  4. At 6 h: $C = 5.71\,e^{-0.1429\times 6} = 5.71\times 0.424 = 2.42$ mg/L (below the MEC of 4 mg/L)
  5. $\text{AUC}_{0\to\infty} = D/CL = 200/5 = 40$ mg·h/L

Check: set the lab to T1, IV bolus, 1 dose and hover at t = 6 h.

2. Repeated dosing to steady state

Same drug, 200 mg every 6 h.

  1. $e^{-k\tau} = e^{-0.857} = 0.424$, so $R_\text{ac} = 1/(1-0.424) = 1.74$
  2. $C_{ss,\max} = 5.71\times 1.74 = 9.93$ mg/L (below the MTC of 12)
  3. $C_{ss,\min} = 9.93\times 0.424 = 4.21$ mg/L (above the MEC of 4)
  4. $C_{ss,\text{avg}} = D/(CL\,\tau) = 200/(5\times 6) = 6.67$ mg/L
  5. Time to ~90% of steady state $\approx 3.3\,t_{1/2} = 16$ h, i.e. roughly the third or fourth dose.

These are the default lab settings: read the steady-state lines and the stats panel.

3. The same regimen in severe renal impairment

T1 is 90% renally cleared ($f_e = 0.9$). Renal function falls to 20% of normal.

  1. $CL = 5\,[0.9\times 0.2 + 0.1\times 1] = 5\times 0.28 = 1.4$ L/h
  2. $k = 1.4/35 = 0.040\ \text{h}^{-1}$, $t_{1/2} = 17.3$ h (3.6× longer)
  3. Unchanged regimen: $C_{ss,\text{avg}} = 200/(1.4\times 6) = 23.8$ mg/L, far above the MTC. $R_\text{ac}$ rises from 1.74 to 4.69.
  4. Keeping $D/\tau$ proportional to $CL$: e.g. 200 mg every 24 h gives $C_{ss,\text{avg}} = 200/(1.4\times 24) = 5.95$ mg/L, but the peak-to-trough ratio $e^{0.04\times 24} = 2.61$ still fits inside a 3-fold window. Try it.

4. Oral dosing of teaching drug T2

T2: $V_d = 50$ L, $CL = 5$ L/h, $k_a = 1.5\ \text{h}^{-1}$, $F = 0.8$, single 400 mg dose.

  1. $k = 0.10\ \text{h}^{-1}$, $t_{1/2} = 6.93$ h
  2. $t_{\text{max}} = \ln(1.5/0.1)/(1.5-0.1) = 2.708/1.4 = 1.93$ h
  3. $C_{\max} = \frac{0.8\times 400\times 1.5}{50\times 1.4}\left(e^{-0.193}-e^{-2.90}\right) = 6.857\times(0.824-0.055) = 5.27$ mg/L
  4. If repeated every 12 h: $C_{ss,\text{avg}} = 0.8\times 400/(5\times 12) = 5.33$ mg/L

5. Loading dose for a long half-life drug (T3)

T3: $V_d = 300$ L, $CL = 6$ L/h, window 1–2 mg/L, target 1.5 mg/L, given IV every 24 h.

  1. $k = 0.02\ \text{h}^{-1}$, $t_{1/2} = 34.7$ h, so steady state takes about 5 days without a loading dose.
  2. Maintenance: $D = C_\text{target}\,CL\,\tau = 1.5\times 6\times 24 = 216$ mg (use 220 mg)
  3. Simple loading dose: $LD = C_\text{target}\,V_d = 1.5\times 300 = 450$ mg gives 1.5 mg/L at once, but by 24 h it has fallen to $1.5\,e^{-0.48} = 0.93$ mg/L, just below the MEC.
  4. With intermittent boluses, load to the steady-state peak instead: $LD = D\,R_\text{ac} = \dfrac{220}{1-e^{-0.02\times 24}} = \dfrac{220}{0.381} \approx 580$ mg. Then $C_0 = 1.93$ mg/L and the first trough is $1.93\times 0.619 = 1.20$ mg/L: inside the window from the start.

6. Competitive antagonist (Schild)

An agonist has $EC_{50} = 1$ µM. A competitive antagonist is present at $[I] = 4K_i$.

  1. Dose ratio $DR = 1 + 4 = 5$, so the apparent $EC_{50}$ is 5 µM.
  2. $E_{\max}$ is unchanged. With a non-competitive antagonist at the same $[I]/K_i$, $E_{\max}$ would fall to $100/5 = 20\%$.

Guided experiments

  1. Half-life rule. T1, 1 dose IV bolus. Hover the curve at 0, 4.85, 9.7 and 14.6 h. Each value should be half the one before.
  2. Dose rate, not dose size. Compare 200 mg every 6 h with 400 mg every 12 h and 100 mg every 3 h. $C_{ss,\text{avg}}$ stays 6.67 mg/L but the swing changes.
  3. Route matters for the shape, not the AUC. Switch T2 between IV bolus and oral with F = 1. The AUC is identical; the oral peak is lower and later.
  4. Distribution phase. Load T4 (two-compartment) and give one dose. Note the rapid early fall (α phase) and the slower terminal phase (β). Increase Q: distribution gets faster.
  5. Renal impairment. With T1 at the default regimen, step renal function from Normal to Severe. Watch the dashed "normal function" curve and the accumulation factor.
  6. Potency vs efficacy. In Dose–response mode, drag agonist B's dot left (more potent) and down (less efficacious).
