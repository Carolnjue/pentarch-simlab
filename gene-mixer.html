1. Genes, alleles and genotypes

A gene sits at a fixed position (locus) on a chromosome. Diploid organisms carry two copies of each autosome, so they have two alleles of each autosomal gene, one from each parent. Two identical alleles make a homozygote (RR or rr); two different alleles a heterozygote (Rr). The genotype is the allele combination; the phenotype is the observable trait.

2. Mendel’s laws

  • Segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate during meiosis, so each gamete carries one allele, with probability ½ each.
  • Independent assortment: alleles of genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on one) are distributed to gametes independently. An individual heterozygous at $n$ such genes makes $2^n$ gamete types in equal proportions.
$$\text{gamete types}=2^n,\qquad \text{Punnett cells}=4^n,\qquad \text{monohybrid } 3{:}1,\ \text{dihybrid } 9{:}3{:}3{:}1,\ \text{trihybrid } 27{:}9{:}9{:}9{:}3{:}3{:}3{:}1$$

Probabilities multiply for independent events: from RrYy × RrYy, $P(\text{wrinkled, green}) = P(rr)\,P(yy) = \tfrac14\times\tfrac14 = \tfrac1{16}$.

4. Sex linkage

Females are XX and males XY (in humans and Drosophila). A male has one X, inherited from his mother, so he is hemizygous for X-linked genes and expresses a recessive X allele. A carrier mother ($X^AX^a$) passes $X^a$ to half her sons, who are affected, and to half her daughters, who are carriers. An affected father passes his X to all daughters and to no sons (no male-to-male transmission).

5. Linkage and recombination

Genes close together on one chromosome tend to stay together. Crossing over in meiosis I produces recombinant gametes at a frequency that grows with distance:

$$RF = \frac{\text{recombinant offspring}}{\text{total offspring}}\times 100\%,\qquad 1\% \text{ RF} \approx 1\ \text{cM},\qquad 0 \le RF \le 50\%$$

With RF = $r$, a dihybrid in coupling (AB/ab) makes gametes AB and ab with frequency $(1-r)/2$ each and Ab, aB with $r/2$ each. In a test cross these frequencies appear directly in the offspring.

6. Testing a ratio: chi-square

$$\chi^2=\sum_i \frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i},\qquad \text{df}=k-1$$

$O_i$ and $E_i$ are observed and expected counts in each of $k$ classes. The p-value is the probability of a deviation at least this large if the hypothesis is true. If $p > 0.05$, the data are consistent with the expected ratio (we do not reject it). Expected counts should be at least 5 for the test to be reliable.

7. Population genetics

In a large, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation or migration, allele and genotype frequencies stay constant (Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium):

$$p+q=1,\qquad p^2+2pq+q^2=1$$

With fitnesses $w_{AA}, w_{Aa}, w_{aa}$, selection changes p each generation:

$$p' = \frac{p^2 w_{AA} + pq\,w_{Aa}}{\bar w},\qquad \bar w = p^2 w_{AA}+2pq\,w_{Aa}+q^2 w_{aa}$$

When heterozygotes are fittest ($w_{AA}=1-s$, $w_{Aa}=1$, $w_{aa}=1-t$), both alleles are kept at $\hat q = s/(s+t)$: the explanation for high HbS frequencies where malaria is common. In small populations, genetic drift adds random change with variance $pq/2N$ per generation, and eventually fixes or loses alleles.

Example 1: Mendel’s dihybrid data

Mendel crossed RrYy × RrYy and counted 556 seeds: 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow, 32 wrinkled green.

  1. Expected 9:3:3:1 of 556: $312.75,\ 104.25,\ 104.25,\ 34.75$.
  2. $\chi^2 = \frac{2.25^2}{312.75}+\frac{3.75^2}{104.25}+\frac{3.25^2}{104.25}+\frac{2.75^2}{34.75} = 0.016+0.135+0.101+0.218 = 0.470$.
  3. df $=4-1=3$; $p = 0.925$. The data fit 9:3:3:1 very well. Load the dihybrid pea preset with 556 offspring and compare.

Example 2: a colour-blind son

Mother $X^BX^b$ (carrier, normal vision) × father $X^BY$.

  1. Mother’s eggs: $X^B$ or $X^b$ (½ each). Father’s sperm: $X^B$ or $Y$ (½ each).
  2. Daughters: $X^BX^B$ or $X^BX^b$: all normal vision, half carriers.
  3. Sons: $X^BY$ or $X^bY$: half colour-blind. Overall: ¼ of all children are colour-blind sons.

Example 3: ABO from two heterozygotes

$I^Ai \times I^Bi$ gives $I^AI^B$, $I^Ai$, $I^Bi$, $ii$ in equal proportions: blood groups AB, A, B and O, ratio 1:1:1:1. Parents with groups A and B can have a child of any group.

Example 4: Labrador coat colour (9:3:4)

  1. BbEe × BbEe gives 9 B_E_ : 3 bbE_ : 3 B_ee : 1 bbee.
  2. E_ dogs deposit pigment in the coat: B_E_ black, bbE_ chocolate.
  3. ee dogs are yellow whatever their B alleles: 3 + 1 = 4. Ratio 9 black : 3 chocolate : 4 yellow. B still shows in the nose: B_ee yellow dogs have black noses, bbee have liver noses.

Example 5: mapping two fly genes

Female $b^+vg^+/b\,vg$ × male $b\,vg/b\,vg$ (test cross), with RF = 17%:

  1. Female gametes: $b^+vg^+$ 41.5%, $b\,vg$ 41.5% (parental); $b^+vg$ 8.5%, $b\,vg^+$ 8.5% (recombinant). The male gives only $b\,vg$.
  2. In 1000 offspring expect ≈ 415 grey long, 415 black vestigial, 85 grey vestigial, 85 black long.
  3. RF = (85 + 85)/1000 = 17%: the genes are about 17 cM apart. (On the standard map they are 18.5 cM apart; double crossovers make RF underestimate long distances.)

Example 6: sickle-cell carriers in a population

If 1% of babies have sickle-cell disease (SS), $q^2 = 0.01$, so $q = 0.1$ and $p = 0.9$.

  1. Carriers: $2pq = 2(0.9)(0.1) = 0.18$, i.e. 18% of people.
  2. Chance two random parents are both carriers: $0.18^2 = 0.0324$; chance their child has SS: $0.0324 \times \tfrac14 = 0.0081$ ≈ 1% (consistent).
  3. With heterozygote advantage $s=0.15$ (AA, malaria) and $t=0.8$ (SS), $\hat q = 0.15/0.95 = 0.158$. Try the preset in the Population view.

Guided experiments

  1. Mendel’s monohybrid. Pea: RR × rr. Breed two F1 plants together. What fraction of F2 seeds are wrinkled? Repeat with 1000 offspring and watch χ² behave.
  2. Hidden alleles. Breed F2 round seeds with the recessive tester. Which round plants were Rr?
  3. Morgan’s flies. Cross a red-eyed female with a white-eyed male, then breed the F1. Which sex are the white-eyed F2 flies? Now try the reciprocal cross.
  4. Linkage. Fly test cross with b and vg. Set RF to 0, 17 and 50%. When do the four classes become equal, and why?
  5. Lethal ratios. Cross two curly-tailed Glimmerpuffs. Why 2:1 and not 3:1? Look for the hatched cells in the Punnett square.
  6. Drift vs size. Population view: run neutral drift with N = 10 and N = 500. How many replicate populations lose or fix A?
