Radio formats

A station’s format is its promise to the listener: what music, how much talk, what tone. Common formats include CHR (contemporary hit radio: current hits, high energy), AC (adult contemporary: familiar songs, warm presentation), talk/news, urban (Afrobeats, amapiano, hip-hop, R&B), gospel and vernacular community stations. Breakfast shows on music stations mix all of these: music, news, travel, ads, competitions and listener calls.

Backtiming

The news joins at a fixed time, so a presenter works backwards from it. If the junction is at $T_J$ and the remaining items have durations $d_i$, the latest moment the first of them may start is

$$t_{start} = T_J - \sum_i d_i$$

If you start later, you will be over and must fade a song early or drop an item; if earlier, you are under and must fill with talk or a short song. The playout log shows each item’s ETA, and the clock’s inner ring draws the rest of the log so you can see at a glance whether it lands on the hour.

Talking up to the post

Most songs have an instrumental intro before the vocals start. That moment is the post. Presenters talk over the intro and finish their sentence just before the post, so the singer comes in as they stop. Talking over the vocals (“crashing the post”) sounds sloppy; finishing ten seconds early leaves an awkward gap. The playout shows an intro countdown for every song.

Live assist and segues

In live assist the playout system loads items but the presenter starts each one. A segue starts the next item as the current one ends; a crossfade overlaps them, usually on a song that fades out. A cold-ending song needs a tight start, or there is a gap. More than about three seconds of silence is dead air, the cardinal sin of radio.

Microphone technique

  • Distance and angle. Work about a hand’s width (10–15 cm) from a dynamic broadcast mic, slightly off-axis so plosives (“p”, “b”) do not thump. Closer gives more bass (the proximity effect) and a more intimate sound; further gives more room.
  • Consistent level. Leaning back or turning to a guest drops your level. Your technical operator, or you when self-opping, rides the fader to compensate.
  • Mic discipline. Assume every mic is live. Open it only when you are about to speak, close it when you finish, and never say anything near an open fader that you would not say on air.
  • Cough key. Hold it to mute your mic for a moment without closing the channel.
  • Speaker cut. When a studio mic opens, the monitor speakers mute so the mic does not pick them up and howl (acoustic feedback). Presenters then hear the programme in headphones.
  • Talk to one person. Radio is intimate. “You” rather than “all of you out there”.
  • Smile. It changes the shape of your mouth and is audible as warmth.
The simulator can use your real microphone if you allow it. Use headphones, or keep the studio speakers set to cut when the mic opens, exactly as a real studio does.

Links that work

  1. Back-announce what just played, briefly.
  2. Say one thing of interest: time check, weather, a listener’s message, a trail for what is coming.
  3. Forward-announce or tease the next item, then hit the next song on your last word, or talk it up to the post.

Levels: meters and alignment

Digital desks meter in dBFS, decibels relative to digital full scale (0 dBFS is the largest possible sample; anything louder clips). Broadcast alignment puts line-up tone at −18 dBFS (EBU R68), which leaves 18 dB of headroom for peaks.

$$L_{dB} = 20\log_{10}\frac{A}{A_{ref}}\qquad -6\ \text{dB} \Rightarrow \tfrac{1}{2}\ \text{amplitude}$$
  • PPM (peak programme meter) reads fast peaks and falls slowly. Speech and music should peak around −10 to −4 dBFS on this desk; never touch 0.
  • Loudness (LUFS) is what listeners perceive. Speech and music should sound equally loud: when you open your mic over a song’s intro, the music usually needs dipping a few dB so you sit on top of it.
  • Processing. Stations run their output through an audio processor (multiband compression and limiting) before the transmitter, which evens out levels. It cannot fix a mic left closed, a missed segue or a distorted, clipped source.

The desk channel

Every channel strip has an ON button (routes it to programme), a fader (level), PFL (listen before air) and a meter. Microphone channels light the studio ON AIR light and cut the speakers. The phone channel carries callers from the telephone balance unit (TBU), band-limited to roughly 300–3400 Hz, so it always sounds thinner than a studio mic.

Silence detection and the delay

Transmission chains have a silence detector: if programme falls below a threshold for a set time (often 10–30 s), it switches to a backup source automatically. Do not wait for it. The profanity delay holds programme for a few seconds (7 s here) so that a producer can dump anything unbroadcastable before it reaches listeners. After a dump the delay has to rebuild, so for a while you are less protected.

Licensing basics

The details vary by country, so treat this as a general guide and check your own regulator’s rules.

  • Broadcasting licence. Issued by the national regulator (for example, the Communications Authority of Kenya, or Ofcom in the UK). It sets the frequency and coverage area and often carries conditions about format, local content and station identification.
  • Music licences. Playing recorded music on air needs licences for two rights: the composition (songwriters and publishers) and the sound recording (producers and performers). Stations usually pay blanket fees to collecting societies and must keep accurate playout logs (music returns) so royalties reach the right artists.
  • Advertising. Ads must be clearly separated from editorial content, follow advertising standards, and air as booked: a dropped or clipped ad is a commercial and compliance problem.
  • Logging. Stations record everything they broadcast (compliance logging) and keep it for a period set by the regulator, so complaints can be checked.

A generic broadcasting code

Harm and offence. Protect audiences, especially children at breakfast and after-school times, from offensive language and harmful content. The profanity delay exists for exactly this.
Accuracy and impartiality. News must be accurate, and opinions on contested public matters should be balanced across the programme.
Fairness and privacy. Do not broadcast someone’s private information, or call people on air, without consent.
Identification and transparency. Identify the station regularly; label sponsorship and paid content clearly; run competitions fairly.
If something offensive does reach air: dump if you can, apologise on air promptly and plainly, log the incident and tell your editor. Regulators judge how a station responded, not only what happened.

1. Backtiming to the news

It is 06:56:40. The remaining log before the 07:00:00 news join is: ad break 1:50, link 0:20, song 1:05 and legal ID 0:08. Total

$$1{:}50 + 0{:}20 + 1{:}05 + 0{:}08 = 3{:}23$$

Latest start for the break: $07{:}00{:}00 - 3{:}23 = 06{:}56{:}37$. You are 3 s late, so you will be 3 s over: trim the link to 17 s, or fade the song 3 s early.

2. Talk-up to the post

A song’s intro is 14 s. You need 2 s to hit the button and breathe, so you have about 12 s of talk. At a natural pace of around 2.5 words per second that is roughly 30 words: “It’s six fifty-four, eighteen degrees in Nairobi, traffic slow on Thika Road. Here’s Matatu Ride.” Finish on the post.

3. Dipping the music under your voice

The music sits at 0 dB and peaks at −6 dBFS. Your voice peaks at −8 dBFS. To sit your voice 6 dB above the music, dip the music fader by $(-6) - (-8) + 6 = 8$ dB, to −8 dB. Its amplitude is then $10^{-8/20} = 0.40$ of the original.

4. Delay after a dump

The delay holds 7.0 s. After a dump it drops to 0 and rebuilds at 0.25 s of delay per second (by slightly stretching audio). Time to full protection:

$$t = \frac{7.0\ \text{s}}{0.25} = 28\ \text{s}$$

A second offensive remark 10 s after the first finds only 2.5 s of delay: you have 2.5 s to react, not 7.

5. Ad minutage

Four breaks an hour of 1:50 each give $4 \times 110 = 440$ s $\approx 7.3$ minutes of ads per hour, about 12% of airtime. Many codes cap advertising per hour; check your licence.

    Instrumental music played under speech.

    Cold end
    A song that stops abruptly rather than fading.
    Cough key
    Momentary mute on a mic channel.
    Dead air
    Unintended silence on air.
    Dump
    Drop the contents of the profanity delay so they never reach air.
    Format clock
    The plan of what happens in each part of the hour.
    Junction
    A fixed hand-over point, such as joining network news on the hour.
    Legal ID
    Station identification required by licence conditions.
    Link
    A piece of presenter talk between items.
    Live assist
    Playout mode in which the presenter starts each item.
    PFL
    Pre-fade listen: hear a source in headphones without putting it on air.
    Post
    The point in a song where the vocals start.
    PPM
    Peak programme meter: a fast-rising, slow-falling level meter.
    Segue
    Moving straight from one item into the next.
    Self-op
    A presenter who operates the desk themselves.
    Sweeper
    A short produced station promo played between items.

    Telephone balance unit: interfaces phone lines with the desk.

    Intercom from producer to presenter, heard only in the studio.
