Lab 3 · Two circulations

Systemic and pulmonary circuits

Both ventricles pump the same volume per minute: in the steady state the outputs of the right and left heart must match, or blood would pile up in one circuit. Structure and the path of blood

Deoxygenated blood returns through the superior and inferior venae cavae to the right atrium, passes the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, and is pumped through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary trunk and arteries to the lungs. Oxygenated blood returns in four pulmonary veins to the left atrium, crosses the mitral valve into the thick-walled left ventricle, and is ejected through the aortic valve into the aorta. The atrioventricular (AV) valves are held by chordae tendineae and papillary muscles so they cannot evert; the semilunar valves close passively when arterial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure.

The cardiac cycle

  1. Atrial systole (after the P wave): tops up ventricular filling by about 20 % at rest.
  2. Isovolumetric contraction (starts just after the QRS): ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, closing the AV valves (S1, "lub"); all valves are shut and volume is constant.
  3. Ejection: when LV pressure exceeds aortic pressure (≈ 80 mmHg) the aortic valve opens; pressure peaks at ≈ 120 mmHg.
  4. Isovolumetric relaxation (after the T wave): LV pressure falls below aortic pressure, the aortic valve closes (S2, "dub", with the dicrotic notch on the aortic trace).
  5. Ventricular filling: when LV pressure falls below LA pressure the mitral valve opens; rapid then slow filling (diastasis).

Cardiac output, stroke volume and ejection fraction

$$SV = EDV - ESV, \qquad CO = HR \times SV, \qquad EF = \frac{SV}{EDV}\times 100\%$$
$$MAP \approx DBP + \tfrac13\,(SBP - DBP), \qquad MAP \approx CO \times SVR$$

Typical adult values at rest: HR 60–100 bpm, SV ≈ 70 ml, CO ≈ 5 L/min, EF 55–70 %. Stroke volume depends on preload (filling; the Frank–Starling law: a more stretched ventricle contracts more strongly), afterload (the pressure it ejects against) and contractility. At very high heart rates diastole shortens so much that filling, and therefore stroke volume, falls.

The model behind the labs

The left ventricle is a time-varying elastance: $P_{LV}(t) = E(t)\,[V_{LV}(t) - V_0]$, where $E(t)$ rises from a diastolic value $E_{min}$ to a systolic peak $E_{max}$ after each ventricular activation (a normalised "double-Hill" activation curve). The left atrium is a smaller elastance with its own contraction, fed from the pulmonary veins. Valves are one-way resistors, and the arteries are a three-element Windkessel (characteristic impedance, compliance $C$ and systemic resistance $R$): $C\,\dfrac{dP}{dt} = Q_{AV} - \dfrac{P}{R}$. The right heart is assumed to move the same volume with the same timing. The model has no baroreflex or autonomic control, so it shows the direct mechanical effect of each rhythm.

Electrical conduction and the ECG

The sinoatrial (SA) node fires spontaneously (≈ 60–100/min). The impulse spreads across the atria (P wave), is delayed ≈ 0.1 s in the atrioventricular (AV) node so the atria can empty, then races down the bundle of His, the left and right bundle branches and the Purkinje fibres (QRS complex, < 0.12 s). Ventricular repolarisation gives the T wave. Normal PR interval 0.12–0.20 s; normal QRS duration < 0.12 s; heart rate from a standard ECG at 25 mm/s is $300 \div$ (number of large squares between R waves).

1. Cardiac output and ejection fraction

  1. EDV = 130 ml, ESV = 55 ml: $SV = 130 - 55 = 75$ ml.
  2. HR = 72 bpm: $CO = 72 \times 75 = 5400$ ml/min $= 5.4$ L/min.
  3. $EF = 75/130 = 58\%$: normal. Compare the live values in Lab 2.

2. Mean arterial pressure and resistance

  1. BP 120/80: $MAP \approx 80 + (120-80)/3 = 93$ mmHg.
  2. With CO = 5.4 L/min = 90 ml/s and right atrial pressure ≈ 0: $SVR = 93/90 = 1.03$ mmHg·s/ml (≈ 1380 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵ after multiplying by 1333).

3. Why tachycardia does not raise output for ever

  1. At 72 bpm each beat lasts 0.83 s; systole ≈ 0.3 s, diastole ≈ 0.53 s.
  2. At 180 bpm each beat lasts 0.33 s; systole shortens only to ≈ 0.2 s, leaving ≈ 0.13 s to fill.
  3. In the model SV falls from about 85 ml to about 40 ml, so CO barely changes even though rate has more than doubled (set 180 bpm and see).

4. Heart rate from an ECG

  1. At 25 mm/s one large square (5 mm) = 0.2 s.
  2. R–R = 4 large squares = 0.8 s: $HR = 60/0.8 = 75$ bpm (or 300 ÷ 4).
  3. PR = 1.5 large squares = 0.30 s > 0.20 s: first-degree AV block (try the preset).
